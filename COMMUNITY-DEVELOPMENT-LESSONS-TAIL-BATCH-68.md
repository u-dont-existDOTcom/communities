## Empirical findings

### 1. Multi-level cooperative credit made mutual liability a movement-scale infrastructure

Before the mid-1980s crisis, Israeli cooperative agriculture used financial cooperation at several levels. Moshav associations borrowed and lent to members; regional purchasing/supply cooperatives borrowed for member settlements and regional enterprises; kibbutzim also relied on movement-level facilities.

A 1989 World Bank working paper by Yoav Kislev, Zvi Lerman, and Pinhas Zusman describes mutual liability/guarantee as a central risk-pooling device. At higher-order regional cooperatives, the same organizations that supplied inputs and other services also became major credit intermediaries. The authors explicitly note that size, complexity, and organizational remoteness created control problems that were much smaller at the local cooperative level.

A later QJE study by Ran Abramitzky describes the pre-crisis kibbutz system as sharing assets/corporations and using movement funds under which kibbutzim could be liable for movement debt in addition to their own obligations.

These arrangements had real benefits: they pooled risk, substituted for conventional collateral, widened access to credit, and supported rapid cooperative development.

Sources:

- Kislev, Lerman & Zusman, `Credit Cooperatives in Israeli Agriculture`, World Bank Policy Research Working Paper 156, 1989.
- Abramitzky, `The Limits of Equality: Insights from the Israeli Kibbutz`, Quarterly Journal of Economics 123(3), 2008.
- Rosenthal & Eiges, `Agricultural Cooperatives in Israel`, Journal of Rural Cooperation 42(1), 2014.

### 2. The same coupling transmitted and amplified the crisis

The 1985 anti-inflation stabilization sharply changed real borrowing conditions after a period of easy credit and overexpansion. Cooperative agriculture accumulated debts it could no longer service.

The World Bank analysis describes higher-order supply cooperatives as deeply connected to regional enterprises through both ownership and credit. Some supply cooperatives became financiers of last resort for failing regional enterprises, financing operating losses and debt service. When service enterprises failed, they could pull supply cooperatives down with them.

The important mechanism is not simply `debt is risky`. **An organization whose direct service function was useful to many communities had also accumulated a second function—financial intermediation backed by mutual liability—so failure in one layer threatened the other.**

Abramitzky likewise records the pre-crisis movement-wide guarantee exposure and the asymmetry of the later shock across kibbutzim.

### 3. Crisis reforms separated service functions from credit intermediation

The 1989 World Bank working paper reports a proposed/required structural safeguard for assisted moshav-sector cooperatives: supply cooperatives were to return to input supply and product marketing and stop financial intermediation; failing regional enterprises were to be closed or sold; moshavim were to deal more directly with banks.

This is a close empirical analogue of G-024's federation-service modularity principle: do not let a critical service layer quietly become an unlimited financial-risk conduit merely because the same federation can technically perform both functions.

### 4. Mutual guarantees were progressively constrained and ultimately dismantled in the kibbutz settlements

The historical literature uses somewhat different levels of aggregation to describe the settlement sequence, so this batch avoids claiming one single clean switch date for every cooperative layer.

The robust sequence is:

- pre-crisis kibbutz/movement finance used broad mutual guarantees;
- late-1980s arrangements constrained the extent to which every kibbutz would simply absorb the whole movement's liabilities and assigned more responsibility according to each kibbutz's economic circumstances;
- Rosenthal & Eiges' 2014 review says the first kibbutz settlement still allocated some mutual-guarantee burden even to kibbutzim that had not accumulated the underlying debt;
- their review says the 1996 second settlement dismantled the mutual-guarantee system and recalculated repayment capacity by kibbutz.

Thus the lesson is not `solidarity was abandoned`. The system changed **which support instruments generated enforceable contingent liabilities across autonomous communities**.

### 5. Post-crisis shared enterprises still needed explicit exit/fixed-cost rules

Rosenthal & Eiges report another post-crisis design problem: after mutual guarantees were dismantled, regional enterprises needed stronger owner commitments because if one kibbutz stopped using/participating in an enterprise and its cost share simply shifted to the remaining kibbutzim, that could encourage additional exits and threaten the enterprise.

The reported response was to require owners to remain responsible for their fraction of fixed costs under defined conditions even if they chose to leave the enterprise.

This does **not** mean every shared service should impose permanent exit debt. It demonstrates the separate accounting question: a member's responsibility for already-committed fixed costs is not the same thing as guaranteeing every other member's borrowing.

## G-027 evidence update — solidarity is not unlimited joint liability

Add a federation support-instrument taxonomy before using the phrase `mutual aid`:

1. **Grant / direct aid** — no repayment claim.
2. **Pooled reserve / insurance-like protection** — defined covered events, contributions, reserves, eligibility, limits, and claims process.
3. **Loan** — repayment obligation belongs to the named borrower under stated terms.
4. **Guarantee / surety** — one community acquires a contingent liability if another cannot pay; this must never be inferred from general federation solidarity.
5. **Shared-enterprise capital/fixed-cost commitment** — responsibility for a collectively approved asset/service and its committed costs, with explicit exit/termination rules.
6. **Non-transactional exchange / learning / relationship transfer** — no bilateral debt unless separately agreed.

Operational controls:

- make guarantee authority explicit, bounded, visible, and separately approved;
- publish gross and contingent exposure by community and by shared institution;
- cap or collateralize guarantees where appropriate;
- do not let `mutual aid` language silently authorize borrowing on behalf of other communities;
- do not retroactively convert a grant into debt because a recipient later recovers;
- distinguish already-committed shared fixed costs from future use obligations;
- define exit/termination consequences before a shared enterprise is built;
- stress-test whether simultaneous shocks make guarantee calls exceed federation liquidity.

The FEC evidence in Batch 66 and the kibbutz evidence here point in the same direction from opposite sides: **a federation can be too transactional for aid, but it can also make solidarity dangerously transactional by turning it into broad enforceable liability.**

## G-024 evidence update — separate services from financial-risk conduits

A shared service institution should have only the financing powers needed for its service unless broader powers are separately justified.

For each function-specific federation institution, distinguish:

- service operations;
- ownership/capital commitments;
- ordinary accounts payable;
- lending/borrowing;
- guarantee/surety authority;
- pooled-risk claims;
- emergency grant authority.

If a service cooperative is allowed to intermediate credit or guarantee debts, that is a separate risk-bearing function with separate caps, reserves, monitoring, and failure isolation.

The 1989 World Bank reform proposal to remove financial intermediation from supply cooperatives after the Israeli crisis is direct empirical support for this distinction.
