## Direct answer: the gap bank was not the final report

The research needed this additional pass.

The [article-gap bank](COMMUNITIES-ARTICLE-GAP-BANK.md) is a strong editorial control document. Its 18 rows ask what each cluster of evidence implies for claims already present in the community article. That is exactly what a later article revision needs. It is not, however, a neutral account of everything the research learned, because the article's current headings and propositions determine the questions, the grouping, and the destination of the evidence.

The distinction is visible in the files. The gap bank contains 660 finding references and reaches 183 of the 186 findings, but compresses them into 18 article-facing rows plus verification and non-promotion notes. Three findings do not appear anywhere in it: F-027 on Shaker communities as de facto child-care institutions, F-030 on an access-rich Rajneeshpuram account overtaken by later events, and F-032 on historical communal waves not establishing a present-day surge. More importantly, the same finding can recur under many gaps while the deeper mechanism linking those gaps remains implicit.

This report reverses the direction. It begins with all 186 findings, groups them horizontally, preserves counterevidence and negative results, and only then asks what the result means for the article. The [finding crosswalk](COMMUNITIES-SYNTHESIS-CROSSWALK.csv) accounts for every row. The [evidence ledger](COMMUNITIES-EVIDENCE-LEDGER.csv) remains the authority for source facts, limits, alternative interpretations, outcomes, and verification needs.

## Scope and evidentiary limits

The completed research contains:

- 984 *Communal Societies* journal PDFs across volumes 1-45;
- eight standalone substantive sources;
- 20 bounded adjacent records covering child response, assessment and review, durable treatment and transition, official correction, and fair separation, pooled risk, and planned fission;
- 443 relevant or contextual journal close reads; and
- 186 promoted findings.

The ledger assigns 88 findings high confidence, 22 medium-high, 67 medium, two medium-low, three low-medium, and four low. It marks 124 for external verification and 62 as not needing it at the present checkpoint. “External verification needed” does not mean a finding is false. It commonly means that a review-level source, underlying court record, current policy, implementation record, or later outcome still needs checking before publication-facing reliance.

Twenty-two findings are bounded searches that did not locate the specified persistent-dangerous-child response sequence in volumes 1-45 or the eight standalone sources. They are evidence about this search and corpus, not proof that no relevant case exists anywhere.

The source base is historically rich but not a representative sample of all communities, harms, or successful designs. Many records are qualitative, retrospective, participant-adjacent, institutionally produced, or focused on unusual conflicts. The adjacent studies add controlled comparisons and authoritative guidance, but they concern professional clinical, child-welfare, residential, legal, or regulatory settings. They do not become intentional-community outcome evidence merely because some functions look similar.

Finding counts in this report show coded coverage. They are not prevalence estimates, effect sizes, or votes. A bounded-search row may summarize dozens or hundreds of PDFs, while several findings may come from one case. No claim below rests on arithmetic alone.

## Executive synthesis

The corpus does not identify a single best community form. It identifies a recurring systems failure.

Communities become dangerous or uncorrectable when legitimate authority in one function—spiritual teaching, therapy, property custody, financial expertise, child care, security, productive skill, public reputation, or founder stewardship—can be converted into control over unrelated functions, including the evidence used to challenge that authority. Once the same person or coalition can define the problem, classify the person, control records, impose consequences, limit outside contact, and make exit materially or relationally costly, democratic forms and sincere ideals provide little protection.

The common failure sequence is:

`legitimate function or dependency → role fusion → evidence and remedy capture → stigmatizing classification or retaliation → costly exit → outward cohesion or survival misread as success → crisis and partial outside correction`

The strongest counterdesign is not leaderlessness, bureaucracy, or state control of ordinary life. It is a modular arrangement in which authority is explicit, bounded by function, replaceable, separated from review of its own conduct, constrained by direct individual rights, and tested by later human outcomes. Communities can retain wide authority over voluntary membership, shared life, ordinary conflict, mutual aid, and culture while preserving independent routes for evidence, appeal, medicine, child safeguarding, law, and exit.

That combined architecture is synthesis claim S-15. It is model-assisted: no source in this corpus validates the whole package as one system. Its components have different evidentiary status and must remain separately testable.

## 1. Authority must be decomposed, not merely democratized — T-01, S-01

The deepest recurring problem is role fusion.

Formal councils, assemblies, co-trustees, elected officers, consultation, and spiritual equality can coexist with practical hierarchy. A council remained subject to founder approval at Brotherhood of the Sun (F-004). Ralahine members could vote on many matters while the landlord-founder retained admissions, officer, business, and first-year expulsion powers (F-034). Ananda had an elected Village Council while an informal council and founder exception held higher practical authority (F-073). The Family International multiplied channels, editors, boards, and local councils while Maria retained control of questions, publication, and final approval (F-085). At Wilderland, title moved to a trust while lifetime trusteeship and appointment power preserved founder control (F-110).

The same mechanism appears outside the founder role. A trusted outside business manager gained practical control of elderly members' assets (F-120). Professional, legal, medical, municipal, and media positions could become gatekeeping points (F-102, F-126, F-137). At Kerista, the same people could be lovers, supervisors, executives, complainants, and evaluators (F-143, F-147). In the Sullivanian material, therapy, intimate relationships, professional supervision, family contact, custody narratives, and complaint evidence converged in one authority chain (F-156, F-180).

The sources therefore support S-01: authority must be mapped and bounded by function. A role map must ask who controls admission, information, agenda, appointments, title, accounts, work, care, discipline, emergency action, family contact, evidence, publication, appeal, and succession. A board is not independent merely because it has a different name. An override is not real until it can operate against the founder or official whose conduct is challenged.

The corpus does not show that all centralization is harmful. One comparative study associated centralization with survival (F-060), and stricter exclusion sometimes appeared stabilizing (F-059). Those findings matter. They show that coordination and decisive authority can solve real collective-action problems. They do not show that survival equals safety, that exclusion identifies the right person, or that concentrated authority should review itself.

A useful counterexample comes from the traditional-society lane. In the !Kung allocation record, productive hunting skill did not automatically confer the right to allocate or receive primary credit for meat (F-165). The practice is not a blueprint for a modern community, but its functional insight transfers: contribution and authority can be deliberately decoupled.

## 2. Participation is not an override; evidence is a governance function — T-02, S-02, S-09

Communities often had abundant speech while lacking a protected way to contradict power.

Modern Times used informal refusal of trade and speech to drive out people regarded as undesirable, including a woman whose dress and publicity offended local norms (F-001, F-002). Former insiders and dissenters were framed as traitors, apostates, wolves, deceivers, lunatics, spies, or agents (F-010, F-037, F-055, F-091, F-133, F-145, F-157). A ritual could produce public unanimity while participants were pressured never to revise their testimony (F-133). Separately generated reports were treated as corroboration even when they arose inside the same belief and authority system (F-081). A mandatory complaint forum could become a bullying mechanism, and an outside evaluator could receive managed or contaminated accounts (F-147).

Control of the record persists beyond the original institution. Oneida archival material was reportedly destroyed by successor-company employees (F-124). Jonestown's demographic composition, supervisor structure, and available former-member voices were badly mismatched in early public accounts (F-136). Favorable media narratives could follow institutional press releases while financial, professional, or personal ties went undisclosed (F-137). A participant-adjacent Rajneeshpuram report that described little coercion was overtaken by later events; its editorial framing correctly preserved it as a time-bound document rather than an authoritative assessment (F-030).

The positive components are modest but real. The Shaker periodical protected some internal disagreement, although editorial allocation remained unequal (F-113). Camphill's “reliable interviewing” effort recognized that reporting must be accessible to people with limited communication (F-122). The Bruderhof leaver network created communications, gatherings, archives, and limited material aid outside the parent institution (F-114).

The synthesis is S-02 and S-09: count neither meetings nor speakers. Ask who sets the question, selects participants, protects confidentiality, edits the record, decides credibility, preserves dissent, controls publication, hears appeal, and can reopen a conclusion. A challenged authority cannot select its own reviewer. A person must be able to report directly, obtain and correct records, use communication accommodations, revise prior assent, and reach an outside route without first winning permission from the institution being challenged.

## 3. Membership needs role-specific gates, not character or danger mythology — T-03, S-04

The corpus contains no validation that contribution, productivity, therapeutic fluency, spiritual attainment, long residence, outward conformity, cohesion, prior conviction, poverty, distress, or administrator-defined suitability reliably distinguishes a dangerous person from a safe one.

Historical communities repeatedly converted structural problems into member classifications. Volunteers were blamed for misconduct while lacking formal voice (F-013). Llano dissidents included low contributors and highly skilled hard workers (F-025). New Harmony's contribution records did not support a received narrative about parasitic members (F-087). Whole-home compliance, spiritual allegations, probation, and length of service did not become conduct-specific evidence merely because institutions relied on them (F-085, F-091, F-106). Even professional structured-judgment tools in adjacent secure settings were outcome-specific, time-limited, and fallible: SAVRY separated groups with different short-term recorded violence rates but did not provide individual certainty or show that assessment improved safety (F-173), while START:AV field performance had material reliability and incremental-validity limits (F-174).

The opposite extreme also failed. Open access combined with little exclusion authority coincided with sanitation, violence, or chronic conflict at Morning Star, the Outlaws of Kalalau, Graham Downs, Renaissance, and the contemporary anarchistic case (F-003, F-082, F-153, F-163). The evidence therefore does not recommend indiscriminate inclusion or the abolition of gates.

The defensible distinction is between a person-level essence judgment and a role-specific access decision. Premove simulations can expose coordination problems (F-019). A community can verify qualifications, references, legal constraints, and conduct relevant to a particular role. It can stage access to money, children, medicine, vehicles, private records, or emergency powers. One standalone participant account describes initially withholding childcare duties while observing an applicant's interactions with children (F-164), but it reports no formal rule or outcome and is only an architecture lead.

Strict selection may improve stability while excluding dissent or people who need support (F-059). A prior record can inform a current, conduct-specific plan without becoming permanent identity. The required control is documented relevance, proportional access, supervision, time limits, reply, review, and an alternative path—not a claim that the institution has discovered who the person essentially is.

## 4. Communities need bounded separation authority — T-04, S-03

The corpus rejects both arbitrary expulsion and procedural paralysis.

Where informal status determined sanctions, conformity disputes could become banishment, humiliation, property destruction, or loss of livelihood (F-001, F-002, F-046, F-130). A graduated Amish disciplinary ladder included private contact, explanation, congregational decision, and reintegration, but its review failed when the same leader controlled the allegation and proposed sanction (F-167). Internal discussion could continue while threatening conduct, fear, retaliation, and property conflict remained unresolved because no one had clear authority to order a fair separation (F-163).

The opposite failure appears where communities resisted rules or exclusion in principle. Morning Star's open-access rule, Kalalau's lack of exclusion authority, and the rule-averse New Zealand cases show that refusal to define authority does not prevent power; it leaves urgent decisions to force, popularity, attrition, police crisis, or whoever controls necessities (F-003, F-082, F-153).

Several components point toward a better process. Interhouse built a relationship with mediators before crisis because residents underused strangers introduced after conflict escalated (F-020). Grasmere separated operational and relational forums and used deferral, reversible trials, and scheduled evaluation (F-072). Moora Moora's experience suggests mediation becomes harder after camps have formed (F-093). The !Kung material distinguishes immediate interruption, cooling, mobility, fission, and outside adjudication, although its lethal and historical practices are not transferable recommendations (F-166).

S-03 is therefore bounded separation authority. A modern process needs a conduct threshold; intake and evidence preservation; immediate lawful protection; notice; a person who may decide interim conditions; recusal; independent review; reply and appeal; proportional, time-limited conditions; protected necessities and family contact; neutral valuation and records; and a lawful outside-response trigger. Mediation can repair a relationship. It cannot substitute for evidence testing, safeguarding, accountability, or a final property settlement when the parties do not consent.

## 5. Community care becomes governance when its firewalls fail — T-05, S-05

The research does not support a generic proposition that “the community is the therapist.” It supports community as a potential source of attention, belonging, practical aid, observation, rehearsal, and recovery support. It also shows why peer care cannot possess clinical, disciplinary, custody, intimate, evidentiary, and membership authority at the same time.

In the Unification Church material, disclosure and confession moved upward into personalized accountability (F-007, F-008). Oneida's Mutual Criticism demanded silent acceptance and was used for medical conditions; professional care was delayed or rejected during serious illness (F-056, F-057). Morehouse trained non-therapists to manipulate emotional intensity while its philosophy could reinterpret perceived deprivation as a victim stance (F-074). Kerista's complaint-and-growth forum became part of founder control (F-147). The Sullivanian record links therapy to family severance, custody narratives, multiple relationships, professional supervision, and complaint influence (F-156, F-180). Shaker mortality findings show that a reputation for communal medical knowledge can coexist with worse subgroup outcomes, continued work during illness, crowding, and failure to use established isolation and rest (F-159).

The adjacent positive evidence does not rescue peer therapy as a governing principle. Favorable programs used trained providers, supervision, fidelity controls, family or school coordination, official records, and continuing individualized services. Results were mixed by conduct, setting, comparator, implementation, and time. Teaching-Family homes had better measured criminal conduct during residence but no statistically significant advantage one year after treatment (F-169). CARE reduced some incident categories but not others and reported no post-exit outcome (F-170). SNAP produced several modest advantages while treatment could continue through follow-up (F-171). Long-term MST and treatment foster care results were favorable in specific trials (F-176, F-178), while current FFT and residential iCBT comparisons found no statistically significant advantage over active care on their reported later endpoints (F-177, F-179).

S-05 is a firewall rule. Peer support should be voluntary and confidential; care records should not flow into discipline, work, housing, romance, custody, or membership decisions; refusal should not cost necessities or belonging; and licensed care, safeguarding, medication, restraint, seclusion, custody, and professional risk assessment remain with qualified and lawful systems. Community members may observe conduct, de-escalate within ordinary law, preserve evidence, support affected people, and help execute a qualified plan. They may not self-license clinical or coercive powers.

## 6. Children require direct rights, not derivative membership protections — T-06, S-06

Children cannot rely on the adult premise that membership is voluntary and exit is available.

The corpus found children as peer-group members whose collective could be supportive or harsh (F-017); as dependents exposed to medical refusal, inadequate schooling, collective adult punishment authority, sexual authority, custody concealment, and adult ideological conflict (F-043, F-049, F-075, F-104); as people whose food required an outside override (F-116); as residents denied ordinary privacy in bathing or sleeping spaces (F-149); as children assigned caregiving and labor while separated from family or denied adequate education and care (F-150, F-155); and as policy subjects whose proposed removal to collective boarding was challenged before implementation (F-152).

The Shaker child-admission record is especially important. Children were admitted partly in hopes of solving adult recruitment and labor needs; some became lifelong members, but the source says the vast majority did not remain and supplies no child-wellbeing cohort (F-155). Institutional retention cannot stand in for a child's welfare. Low retention can mean failure, successful refuge, ordinary maturation, family retrieval, harm, or several things at once (F-027).

S-06 is a direct rights floor. It includes food, sleep, medical access, education, privacy, bodily safety, age-appropriate roles, family and outside contact, communication accommodations, independent advocacy, refusal, reply, appeal, lawful custody, portable records and learning, and transition support. These rights do not depend on the child's danger classification, guilt, treatment response, usefulness, family status, or the institution's survival. Collective childrearing can add adults and peers to a child's support system; it cannot dissolve named adult accountability or the child's independent route out of that system.

## 7. Exit is four different rights, and assets determine whether any is real — T-07, S-07, S-08

The research separates four dimensions of exit:

1. **formal permission** to withdraw or be separated;
2. **material usability** through housing, cash, benefits, records, skills, transport, health care, and a realistic timetable;
3. **relational continuity** through family contact, child access, outside ties, and freedom from reputational retaliation; and
4. **procedural vindication** through independent valuation, advice, evidence, mediation where voluntary, adjudication where necessary, and enforceable payment.

A community can satisfy one dimension and fail the others. Long-serving Brotherhood members were expelled without financial help (F-005). Jerusalem Colony members surrendered assets without a return right and allegedly lacked practical means to leave (F-042). Bruderhof and Jesus Fellowship exits could sever family contact or the necessities supplied by membership (F-051, F-067). Harmony dissidents had surrendered earnings and lacked outside skills, making organized separation dependent on an outside sponsor (F-112). Moora Moora promised a buyout but acknowledged that liquidity and timing could still make it unusable (F-092).

Property and financial architecture repeatedly controlled the real constitution. Ralahine's founder owned the land (F-034). Aquarian Foundation assets could be destroyed after leader capture (F-035). Kadarim's accounts lagged by almost two years during a debt crisis (F-047). Founder removal at Zion did not automatically transfer titled assets (F-101). A perpetual trust preserved Renaissance land while collective purpose and buildings deteriorated (F-108). Wilderland's deed separated title without separating trustee appointment power (F-110). A trusted outside manager captured assets through financial custody (F-120). Twin Oaks completed an enforceable occupancy and acquisition transition after rejecting founder leadership (F-139).

The adjacent instruments sharpen the distinction without proving effectiveness. A draft Mothership agreement permits exit and funds a prompt small payment, but its larger benefit can be nonvested, delayed, or discounted and no executed agreement or household outcome was located (F-183). PEACH separates federation governance from general delegates but, in the inspected account, gives no direct claim right to an individual whose home community will not file (F-184). A planned Hutterite split can divide aggregate assets while assigning households by lot, and a court dissolution allocation is not a voluntary fork protocol (F-185). The Jesus Fellowship closure record creates claim categories and finality after collapse but also shows record, deadline, advice-cost, screening, and voting barriers that a pre-crisis exit right should prevent (F-186).

Positive components exist: time away with a route home (F-016), portable training (F-018), accounts and withdrawal formulas (F-021), arbitration or later civil recovery (F-044, F-050), portable pensions (F-054), graduated belonging (F-068), practical household exit support (F-099), leaver networks (F-114), and completed entity/title transition (F-139). No record validates the full chain from a written right through funded household use, protected relationships, neutral review, and later wellbeing.

S-07 and S-08 therefore require rights and controls before crisis: individual vesting, liquid reserves, independent accounts and valuation, dual authorization, appointment and removal rules, former-member records, advice support, escrow, rapid safety exceptions, protected family contact, debt and claim priorities, and later outcome review. A deed is not control. Permission is not usability. A payment is not recovery.

## 8. Material capacity precedes moral diagnosis — T-08, S-12

Communities are not autonomous merely because they govern themselves internally.

Historical cases depended on land, water, soil, transport, markets, legal capacity, credit, schools, health care, utilities, and relations with surrounding institutions (F-024). Llano and Sunrise admitted or recruited beyond their agricultural, financial, or skill capacity and then developed labor conflict, sanctions, and blame (F-025, F-026, F-033). Allensworth built internal civic institutions but remained vulnerable to incorporation rules, rail access, water suppliers, regional diversion, drought, segregation, and markets (F-134). Kibbutz prosperity and stability could depend on state credit and settlement policy, making sponsor withdrawal a separate success risk (F-129). Cross-organization partnership language could suppress conflict while leaving operational ownership unassigned (F-132).

S-12 is a formation and operating rule: map water, food, housing, health capacity, child-care capacity, qualified labor, debt, revenue, seasonal load, legal capacity, transport, utilities, schools, markets, data, suppliers, and emergency alternatives before treating a problem as a character failure. A site or capability budget should include what happens when the strongest outside dependency fails.

This does not make local autonomy meaningless. It makes autonomy specific. A community can choose how it organizes ordinary life while acknowledging nonoptional duties and external choke points. “Selecting couplings” is useful only if it includes the couplings one cannot ethically or legally decline.

## 9. Outside correction is necessary, function-specific, and fallible — T-09, S-10

The corpus neither romanticizes state intervention nor treats it as optional in every domain.

Courts and agencies answer the claim, jurisdiction, evidence, and remedy before them. They may set a property boundary without repairing a relationship (F-036, F-050, F-119), end a criminal or civil case through settlement without a complete accountability record (F-086), or remove an official or licensed authority without producing family repair, survivor recovery, or later institutional safety (F-101, F-180). An official inquiry may document blocked reporting and recommend continuing action without proving implementation (F-181). Police referral and convictions can establish accountability for proven offences without resolving every allegation, redress claim, survivor need, or successor risk (F-182).

Outside systems can also be conflicted, captured, underprepared, or wrong. Local political institutions can reflect a community voting bloc (F-102). Raids can rest on false premises or unreliable channels (F-123). Civil or clinical process can be initiated against a dissenter by leaders who control the narrative (F-145). Media, professionals, regulators, courts, police, schools, and social services all need conflict, evidence, proportionality, and after-action controls (F-062, F-126, F-127, F-137).

The answer is not one generic “outside authority.” S-10 assigns distinct functions:

- emergency services address immediate threats;
- police and courts investigate and adjudicate legally defined offences and claims;
- child-protection and custody systems address lawful child safety and family authority;
- licensed clinicians assess and treat within professional scope;
- regulators govern professional authority;
- inspectors address health, building, fire, labor, or environmental duties;
- independent mediators facilitate voluntary agreement;
- auditors, trustees, arbitrators, and courts handle defined financial or governance disputes;
- survivor and leaver services support people without deciding whether an allegation is legally proven; and
- inquiries preserve systemic evidence and recommend correction.

Each route needs a trigger, accessible intake, evidence preservation, conflicts disclosure, least-restrictive and proportionate action, a stopping rule, appeal where applicable, and later outcome review. Community members retain ordinary legal powers to observe, document, de-escalate, protect immediate safety, and seek help. They do not inherit police, court, clinical, custody, restraint, seclusion, licensing, or regulatory powers.

## 10. Success is a vector, not a score — T-10, S-11

Longevity, size, cohesion, reputation, member fulfillment, environmental practice, founder removal, conviction, license revocation, asset preservation, process completion, and in-program improvement are different endpoints. They can conflict.

Wagner explicitly separated goal attainment, social merit, longevity, size, cohesion, influence, and personal growth (F-014). Cohesion and satisfaction can rate authoritarian groups highly (F-015). A comparative study associated centralization with survival (F-060). Renaissance's perpetual trust preserved land while mission, maintenance, and collective life deteriorated (F-108). A simplified ecological score could add positive practices without subtracting serious harms or measuring resident outcomes (F-140).

The adjacent evidence reinforces the time problem. Teaching-Family advantages during residence did not remain statistically significant one year later (F-169). A staff debrief generated reported learning and some care-plan discussion without significantly reducing event rate or severity (F-175). Residential self-report improvement did not establish added post-release benefit (F-179). License revocation, conviction, an inquiry recommendation, a claims cutoff, or a trust transfer is an administrative or legal endpoint, not a complete human outcome (F-180 through F-186).

S-11 requires an outcome vector with separate, non-nettable dimensions:

- immediate and later safety, by conduct and affected person;
- victim and survivor safety and recovery;
- child wellbeing, education, development, privacy, and family contact;
- resident autonomy, dissent, and direct access to remedy;
- material security, housing, health access, debt, and exit usability;
- relationship continuity and family burden;
- mission performance and ecological or social goals;
- institutional viability and founder replaceability;
- implementation fidelity, adverse effects, false positives, and false negatives; and
- post-exit, post-treatment, post-fission, and post-correction outcomes.

Benefits do not offset non-waivable harms. A community may be productive, loving, ecological, spiritually meaningful, or durable while still failing a safety or rights floor. Dissolution may be adaptive; survival may be failure. The dimensions should be reported separately rather than collapsed into a single success label.

## 11. Continuity can outlive the institution — T-11, S-13

The evidence repeatedly separates the community as a legal institution from the movement of people, practices, and relationships.

Oneida changed marriage rules and converted to a joint-stock form rather than preserving every original institution (F-022). Members and practices moved among nineteenth-century communities before and after dissolution (F-023). A preexisting elected council became the Harmony Society's governing body after founder death (F-079). Amana eventually separated religious and business forms despite a founder text opposing change (F-080). Koreshan successors adapted authority after the founder died (F-098). Zion dissenters left the church, remained residents, created parallel institutions, and later gained civic power (F-103). Bruderhof leavers built a post-exit network and counter-archive (F-114).

Planned fission can also be a continuity mechanism. The Hutterite record supplies a population trigger, years of preparation, a daughter site, asset division, and a parity obligation to the originating colony (F-161). Unit E adds the necessary limit: aggregate parity does not establish individual consent, household choice, child voice, family contact, debt fairness, or both successors' later viability (F-185).

S-13 treats continuity at several levels: person, household, alumni network, resident community, legal entity, land trust, successor, federation, and movement. A founder's death, institutional conversion, fork, migration, or closure need not erase the project. A continuity plan should include replaceable authority, amendment rules, record custody, portable skills and benefits, alumni communication, the ability to remain resident while changing affiliation, voluntary fork procedures, transparent assets and debt, and fixed reviews of both successors.

This is not a presumption that fission is always healthy. A fork can become expulsion, family division, asset capture, or abandonment. Planned fission is different from a court allocation after breakdown, and both are different from an individual's usable right to refuse.

## 12. The persistent-dangerous-child question remains unanswered — T-12, S-14

The most important negative result should remain explicit.

The intentional-community corpus does not answer the persistent-dangerous-child question.

Across volumes 1-45 and the eight standalone sources, 22 bounded-search findings repeatedly found children as victims, dependents, students, workers, custody subjects, peer-group members, ordinary rule-breakers, or subjects of adult policy. They did not locate an intentional-community case documenting the full sequence:

`persistent dangerous conduct by a child → conduct-specific allegation → assessment → intervention → review → later outcome`

That does not prove no community ever handled such a case. Privacy, euphemism, aggregate reporting, source destruction, and routing into family, school, disability, juvenile, medical, or child-protection systems can hide the sequence. It does mean this corpus cannot support a claim that intentional communities have demonstrated a reliable internal solution.

The adjacent unit clarifies functions but does not close the gap:

- Teaching-Family programs reported better criminal-conduct measures during residence but no statistically significant one-year advantage after treatment (F-169).
- CARE changed some setting-level incident trends, not all, with no post-exit outcome (F-170).
- SNAP combined child and caregiver skills, trained providers, individualized continuation, and repeated measures, with several modest favorable results and several limits (F-171).
- NICE guidance separates assessment, safeguarding, de-escalation, least-restrictive intervention, monitoring, debrief, external review, and governing oversight, but is normative guidance rather than a causal evaluation (F-172).
- SAVRY separated short-term institutional-violence risk groups without creating individual certainty or proving management benefit (F-173).
- START:AV results varied by outcome, showed reliability problems, and added no tested predictive value beyond lifetime history for the analyzable outcomes (F-174).
- TALK debriefs produced reported learning but no significant incident-rate or severity reduction and were not independent review (F-175).
- One MST trial found favorable official outcomes more than two decades later (F-176), while an FFT effectiveness trial found no statistically significant advantage over active care on its reported registry endpoints (F-177).
- Treatment foster care outperformed group care on a 24-month delinquency construct in a small bundled trial (F-178), while an added residential iCBT module showed no significant post-release benefit over active care and had low completion (F-179).

No study validates a lay personality or membership screen, transfers professional or statutory authority to an intentional community, or supplies the requested individual intentional-community sequence. The practical synthesis is an interface: a community needs observable-conduct documentation, immediate lawful protection, direct child and caregiver participation, trained independent assessment, a child-rights floor, a qualified plan, fidelity and adverse-effect review, family and school coordination, and post-transition outcomes. Which institution performs each step depends on law and competence; the child cannot be made dependent on the home community's permission to reach it.

## Cross-theme tensions that must not be flattened

The findings do not support a frictionless blueprint. The final architecture must keep these tensions visible:

| Tension | What one side protects | What the other side reveals |
|---|---|---|
| Local autonomy vs outside correction | culture, intimacy, self-government | some functions require independent, licensed, or statutory authority; outside systems can still be captured or wrong (F-102, F-123, F-145) |
| Inclusion vs exclusion | refuge, diversity, second chances | open access without bounded authority can expose residents to persistent danger or unmanageable load (F-003, F-027, F-153, F-163) |
| Decisive authority vs capture | coordination, speed, survival | centralized power can suppress evidence and make itself irremovable (F-060, F-073, F-085, F-097) |
| Solidarity vs portable entitlement | pooled resources and long-term investment | nonvested benefits and community-level gates can make exit or care unusable for the person (F-054, F-183, F-184) |
| Asset lock vs adaptive purpose | protection from privatization | land can survive after mission and maintenance fail, with no humane successor path (F-108) |
| Privacy vs evidence preservation | dignity and trust | secrecy can hide abuse, destroy records, or make later correction impossible (F-124, F-157, F-181) |
| Mediation vs adjudication | voluntary repair | mediation cannot determine contested facts, impose lawful protection, or bind a nonconsenting party (F-020, F-093, F-109) |
| Aggregate parity vs individual consent | viable successor institutions | a balanced split can still force a household or divide family ties (F-161, F-185) |
| Institutional finality vs remedy access | finite funds and closure | deadlines, records, cost, classification, and voting can exclude valid late or difficult claims (F-186) |
| Strong value transmission vs child conscience | cultural continuity | children need a present right to question, reject, and carry portable learning elsewhere (F-029, F-152) |
| In-setting improvement vs durable change | immediate safety and learning | gains may attenuate after structure or services end (F-169, F-175, F-179) |
| Community care vs independent care | belonging and practical support | fused care can control medicine, family, discipline, and evidence (F-056, F-057, F-156, F-159) |

These are design problems, not reasons to abandon community. A safeguard should state which risk it accepts, which risk it reduces, how it will be measured, and who can challenge it.

## The combined architecture — S-15

The strongest synthesis is function-specific subsidiarity with direct individual routes. Each function belongs at the lowest level that can perform it competently without reviewing its own conduct or disabling a person's rights. “Lowest level” does not mean “inside the home community” when the home community is the conflict.

| Function | Local community may do | Independent or federated layer may do | Professional, judicial, or public layer must retain | Direct individual route |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Membership and roles | invite, stage access, define duties, evaluate observable role conduct | hear appeal, check conflicts, maintain intercommunity alternatives | enforce employment, discrimination, contract, or safeguarding law where applicable | obtain terms and records; refuse a role; appeal; leave |
| Ordinary conflict | dialogue, Hearthwork or other voluntary peer support, cooling, reversible trial, ordinary house boundaries | neutral mediation, facilitated meeting, independent review of a separation decision | adjudicate legal claims or immediate danger within lawful scope | choose support; decline compelled disclosure; report outside |
| Serious harm | protect immediate safety within ordinary law, document observable conduct, preserve evidence, call help | survivor support, independent intake, records custody, after-action review | investigate crime, assess clinical risk, order lawful restrictions, determine custody | contact emergency, clinical, legal, advocacy, or safeguarding systems directly |
| Care and medicine | mutual aid, meals, transport, companionship, implement a voluntary qualified plan | pooled financing, patient advocacy, quality and access audit | diagnosis, treatment, medication, restraint, seclusion, licensing, public health | independent care, confidentiality, second opinion, appeal, emergency access |
| Children and family | provide additional caring adults, schooling support, safe routines, age-appropriate participation | independent child advocate, family-contact support, records and outcome audit | child protection, custody, compulsory education and health duties, lawful restrictive care | child-accessible reporting and advocacy; protected family and outside contact |
| Evidence and reporting | log events, preserve originals, distinguish allegation from finding, protect correction | confidential intake, duplicate archives, independent review, accessible communication | subpoena, investigate, adjudicate, regulate, or publish official findings within authority | obtain, correct, and export records; report without community permission |
| Assets and exit | publish accounts, fund reserves, provide transition support, execute ordinary payments | neutral valuation, escrow, audit, pooled risk, leaver network, fork assistance | enforce fiduciary, trust, contract, property, insolvency, pension, and claims law | vested entitlement, advice, records, appeal, emergency withdrawal |
| Succession and fission | elect or replace leaders, amend rules, plan voluntary division | parity audit, successor support, shared records, intercommunity mobility | resolve contested title, debt, fiduciary, family, or legal claims | refuse assignment, preserve household and family ties, choose affiliation |
| Outcomes | collect local safety, wellbeing, participation, and capacity data | compare both successors and leavers, audit missing data and adverse effects | maintain official health, education, justice, licensing, or regulatory outcomes | see relevant results; contest the record; participate in evaluation |

The independent or federated layer is not a new sovereign. It can also be captured. Its authority should be task-specific, time-limited, audited, and bypassable through direct individual routes. Federations are most useful for functions a single community cannot neutrally or economically sustain: appeals, audits, pooled risk, records, training, intercommunity mobility, alumni support, and planned-fission assistance.

## Minimum control set implied by the findings

These are supported components assembled by the synthesis, not a validated package:

1. **A practical power map:** title, accounts, appointments, information, sanctions, emergency powers, family contact, evidence, appeal, and succession—not just an organization chart.
2. **A direct rights floor:** necessities, safety, privacy, care, outside and family contact, education, records, refusal, reply, appeal, advocacy, and lawful external access.
3. **Role-specific staged access:** privileges expand by relevant conduct and competence; person-level essence and danger labels do not substitute for evidence.
4. **A fair-separation protocol:** conduct threshold, intake, immediate protection, recusal, evidence, reply, appeal, proportion, review, and neutral material settlement.
5. **Care firewalls:** peer support remains separate from clinical authority, custody, discipline, romance, work, benefits, and membership records.
6. **Independent truth channels:** accessible intake, confidentiality limits, anti-retaliation, preserved dissent, duplicate records, provenance, correction, and external escalation.
7. **Pre-crisis exit infrastructure:** individual vesting, sufficient liquid reserves, records, advice, housing and care transition, family contact, escrow, and enforceable deadlines.
8. **Asset and fiduciary controls:** title plus appointment, removal, succession, dual authorization, conflicts, data timeliness, audit, debt, and purpose review.
9. **A capacity and dependency budget:** people, land, water, food, housing, health, children, labor, debt, revenue, suppliers, law, transport, education, and emergency alternatives.
10. **A non-nettable outcome dashboard:** safety and rights alongside mission, ecology, relationships, material viability, institutional survival, later outcomes, and adverse effects.
11. **Succession and fission rules:** real leader replacement, record custody, voluntary household choice, transparent asset and debt division, and both-successor review.
12. **Named external interfaces:** purpose, jurisdiction, intake, evidence threshold, conflicts, proportionality, stopping rule, appeal, and after-action review for each outside function.

## What this means for Joel's article and thesis

The gap bank already performs the editorial translation. This report does not rewrite the article, but it clarifies which changes are load-bearing.

The research does not refute Joel's canonical thesis that communal living is a return to our evolved ancestral pattern and that large societies breed anomie and capture by psychopaths. The assigned corpus was not designed to test ancestral evolution, comparative anomie, the prevalence of psychopathy, or whether large societies are more capturable than small ones. Historical conduct is described here without retrospective diagnosis.

What the corpus does establish is the necessary corollary: small scale does not automatically dissolve capture. It can move control into more intimate and consequential domains—food, housing, care, confession, work, sexuality, family contact, children, records, and exit. A community that wants to resist large-system capture must make its own authority modular, correctable, and survivable by the person who challenges it.

Several article ideas remain strongly aligned with the evidence: premove trials, explicit founder replacement, written exit terms, protected childhood development, attention to land and money, reversible experiments, planned endings, and forks. The synthesis makes them more exact.

Four article propositions require the clearest evidentiary boundary:

- **“The community is the therapist.”** Community can be a healing environment and peer-support network; it cannot safely fuse care with discipline, custody, intimate authority, evidence, medicine, or membership. This is a challenge, not a minor addition (G-002; S-05).
- **Long work is hard for con artists to fake.** The corpus validates no commitment, productivity, spiritual, therapeutic, or duration screen for dangerousness. Use staged, role-specific access and conduct evidence instead (G-018; S-04).
- **The community can handle the hardest dangerous-child case internally.** The intentional-community evidence is a bounded null. The adjacent evidence supports an interface with trained and lawful systems, not a transfer of their powers (G-009 and G-018; S-06 and S-14).
- **Legal and medical systems are selected couplings.** Communities can choose providers and institutional forms, but crime, custody, child protection, emergency care, public health, professional licensing, and other duties are not wholly optional (G-013; S-10).

The article-gap bank should therefore remain the change specification, while this report becomes the conceptual source of truth for why those changes relate.

## What remains unknown

The completed corpus and finite adjacent roadmap leave real uncertainties:

1. No intentional-community case supplies the complete persistent-dangerous-child sequence with later outcome.
2. No source tests the combined S-15 architecture as a package.
3. The corpus cannot estimate how common each failure or protective component is across communities.
4. It does not compare intentional communities with nuclear families, ordinary neighborhoods, institutions, or states on a common outcome panel.
5. Many positive components lack adoption, fidelity, adverse-effect, leaver, child, survivor, or long-term outcome data.
6. Many official corrections lack implementation and recovery follow-up.
7. Many exit, pooled-risk, and fission instruments lack executed agreements, audited reserves, actual payouts, refusals, appeals, and household outcomes.
8. The historical-wave finding does not establish a present-day 2024-2026 surge in communal formation or interest (F-032).
9. Evidence-channel performance remains difficult to observe because unreported, destroyed, inaccessible, or institutionally filtered records have no denominator.
10. It remains unknown which minimum set of safeguards produces the most protection with the least bureaucracy, cost, false-positive exclusion, or loss of local autonomy.

These are not reasons to erase the conclusions that do converge. They are the boundary around them. The assigned research program is complete; any further research should begin with a new, finite question rather than reopening the finished corpus indiscriminately.

## Final conclusions

Everything learned can be compressed into twelve decisive conclusions:

1. The recurring danger is not simply a bad leader. It is control that crosses functions and captures its own evidence and correction.
2. Formal democracy, equality, consensus, or consultation is inadequate unless practical veto, appointment, information, and appeal power can be used against the authority being challenged.
3. Communities need real, conduct-specific, reviewable separation authority; the absence of rules can be as unsafe as arbitrary rule.
4. Contribution, skill, charisma, cohesion, conformity, therapeutic fluency, long service, criminal history, poverty, and labels are not validated danger screens.
5. Community care is valuable when it remains support. It becomes governance when it controls medicine, custody, discipline, intimate life, evidence, or membership.
6. Children need direct rights and advocates because adult voluntariness and exit do not protect them.
7. Exit is not a sentence in an agreement. It is permission plus liquidity, records, housing and care continuity, family contact, advice, neutral review, and later usability.
8. Property title, accounts, appointments, records, debt, reserves, and succession are the practical constitution.
9. Independent reporting and external correction are indispensable for some functions, but each outside system needs its own conflict, evidence, proportionality, and outcome controls.
10. Survival, cohesion, legal closure, conviction, leader removal, process completion, and in-program gains are not substitutes for disaggregated human outcomes.
11. A community can preserve purpose through transformation, migration, alumni networks, parallel institutions, succession, or voluntary fission; preserving the original entity is not always the goal.
12. The most defensible combined design is function-specific subsidiarity with direct individual rights and routes. It is the synthesis of supported components, not a proven finished model.
