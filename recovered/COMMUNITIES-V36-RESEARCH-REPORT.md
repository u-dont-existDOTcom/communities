## Main result

Volume 36 adds three narrow lessons: **voice mechanisms must work for people with limited communication, outside intervention needs its own false-positive controls, and preservation of accountability records must survive organizational succession.**

1. **Access to expression is part of governance, not only care.** McKanan reports that Camphill Soltane worked with the Council on Quality and Leadership to train coworkers and employees in “reliable interviewing,” intended to help people with limited communication skills articulate what they value and need. He says this helped the community organize around individual goals. The article does not supply disabled participants' independent evaluations, error rates, confidentiality rules, dispute use, or later safeguarding outcomes. The transferable mechanism is therefore bounded: accessible communication support, chosen by the person and insulated from discipline or membership decisions, should be available before a grievance or safety crisis. (F-122)

2. **An outside actor is not automatically an independent or accurate actor.** Miller's review says Wright and Palmer document dozens of government raids on religious communities and argue that many were based on false premises. The review attributes part of the pathway to self-described “cult experts,” angry former members who receive disproportionate attention, anticult organizations, media uptake, and law-enforcement action. It also notes the catastrophic loss of life at Waco. The underlying book and individual case records were not inspected here, and the review does not show that every raid was unjustified or that serious child-protection allegations should be ignored. The durable challenge is procedural: coercive intervention needs conduct-specific evidence, qualified and conflict-disclosed expertise, proportionality, a defined stopping rule, and post-action review rather than a category label alone. (F-123)

3. **Evidence custody must outlive the community form.** Guiler's review reports that in 1947 Oneida Limited employees, possibly including the book author's grandfather, took G. W. Noyes's Oneida Community archive to a dump and burned it. The reviewer treats the destruction as reported fact but says the participants and motives remain uncertain; the underlying book and the little-known study he mentions were not inspected. Whatever the motive, the reported outcome is irreversible loss of a record set after the commune had become a corporation and descendant community. Preservation therefore needs independent custody, duplication, retention and legal-hold rules, access controls, and a succession plan that binds successor entities rather than relying on the goodwill of current insiders. (F-124)

## Source and interpretation cautions

- F-122 comes from a sympathetic outsider's interview-based article. Inspect the communication method, training materials, consent and confidentiality rules, disabled participants' accounts, and later outcomes before treating it as a validated safeguarding instrument.
- F-123 is a review of a book not inspected in this checkpoint. Verify each raid's predicate, warrant, legal findings, child-safety record, injuries, remedies, and later official review; do not infer that every allegation was false or every external intervention illegitimate.
- F-124 is a review of a descendant-authored book. Verify the 1947 event, archive scope, participants, custody, and any surviving copies in the underlying book, cited study, corporate records, and repositories. The motives listed are speculation, not findings.
- Chavance's account of the 1810 Union Village confrontation relies heavily on Shaker correspondence and a Shaker participant's published narrative. The inspection and immediate de-escalation are useful corroboration, but the original accusations, inspector independence, consent conditions, and individual follow-up require other records.
- The Pinery Saints' exit-property accounts come from participants in factional conflict. They establish that severe forfeiture was claimed and apparently covenanted, not that every person's property, assent, or legal entitlement was identical.
- The ecovillage studies are short observational case studies with selected interview samples. They document practices and reported perceptions, not causal proof that admission, consensus, ownership, or environmental systems were safe or durable.
- Short book reviews identify leads and contrasts, but their compression generally cannot establish a complete allegation, assessment, intervention, review, and later outcome.
