## Main result

Volume 39 adds three narrow lessons: **a founder transition must reach title and occupancy, a success score must not let positive practices erase harms or missing data, and a doctrine of consent cannot override a requested physical safeguard or independent child voice.**

1. **Founder replacement becomes practical only when the institution controls its occupancy and acquisition rights.** Schwenkbeck reports that founding member Bud Lucas bought the Twin Oaks farm, then left after members rejected his attempts at leadership. As the original three-year lease approached expiration, he sought the land back or payment. Twin Oaks had incorporated, sought legal advice, rejected a proposed lease without adequate purchase protection, and negotiated an August 1970 agreement: three additional rent-free years followed by a 25-year purchase at six percent interest, executed for the corporation by two members. The article does not adjudicate motives, establish the legal strength of either side's position, or show that incorporation alone caused the result. It does document a positive control transition: written entity authority, legal review, acceptable occupancy terms, authorized signatures, and a completed path from founder-owned land to institutional purchase. A founder can be rejected politically while retaining a practical veto through title; the override test therefore has to reach land, debt, possession, and enforceable transfer. (F-139)

2. **An additive success score can reward strengths while being structurally unable to subtract harms.** Lopez and Weaver contacted 38 apparent ecological intentional communities, received 13 responses, and obtained nine usable surveys for a pilot environmental classification. Their simplified binary score added a point for each positive practice across five domains. The authors explicitly state that it could reward positive practices but could not penalize negative or harmful ones, and they disclose convenience sampling, self-selection, omitted communities, thin data, restricted domains, and interpretive category boundaries. The study is appropriately presented as preliminary and does not purport to audit interpersonal safety or governance. Its methodological warning transfers beyond its subject: a dashboard needs separate domains, adverse-outcome and non-waivable-harm measures, missing-data disclosure, comparators, and a rule preventing success in one domain from offsetting failure in another. (F-140)

3. **A proclaimed consent doctrine cannot substitute for a requested safeguard, independent voice, or meaningful freedom to refuse.** Coulthard reports that Nashoba's enslaved members could not leave and left no firsthand accounts. After an enslaved man reportedly entered the cabin shared by Nelly and Isabel without invitation and demanded sexual favors, Nelly requested a door lock. James Richardson refused, arguing that the community's doctrine of unconstrained mutual choice offered greater protection; white members defended his position. The source also reports Richardson's relationship with Josephine Prevot, the free black fourteen-year-old daughter of Nashoba's schoolteacher. No independent assessment, child advocate, protective review, appeal, remedy, or individual later outcome is documented for Nelly, Isabel, or Josephine. Coulthard also reports that Wright ultimately emancipated thirty-one people and settled them in Haiti, and interprets that result as a successful phase in Wright's reform work. The audit preserves that interpretation while refusing to net mission benefit against non-waivable safety, consent, voice, and exit failures. (F-141)

## Source and interpretation cautions

- F-139 is a historical reconstruction, not a legal opinion. Inspect the leases, articles of incorporation, board authority, correspondence, deed, land records, and payment history before stating legal effect, bargaining power, or motive.
- F-140 is a deliberately limited environmental pilot. Its disclosed inability to subtract harmful practices is a transferable scoring warning, not evidence that its nine classifications were erroneous or that it measured domains it excluded.
- F-141 relies on historical records largely created or preserved by white participants and later historians; the enslaved members left no known firsthand accounts in the source. Preserve the reported nature of the cabin event and relationship, do not invent Josephine's perspective or specific conduct, and do not convert eventual emancipation into proof that prior participation or relationship rules were voluntary.
- None of the sources supports a retrospective diagnosis of antisocial personality disorder or psychopathy. Findings describe reported conduct, institutional structure, evidence limits, response processes, and outcomes.
