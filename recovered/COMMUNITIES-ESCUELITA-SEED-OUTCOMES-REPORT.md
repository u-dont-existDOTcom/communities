# Did the Escuelita's seeds sprout?

Date: 2026-08-15 (Africa/Dakar)
Status: completed bounded research unit; no article prose revised
Evidence added: F-193 through F-198

## Direct answer

Yes, but mostly as **networks, political education, solidarity practice, and adaptations inside existing work**. The evidence is much weaker for the stronger thing Joel is looking for: a new, durable community or autonomous governing institution founded because of the 2013-2014 Escuelita and shown to improve how people live over time.

The most defensible result is:

- alumni became recognizable collective actors in several places and continued acting after the school;
- participants report carrying principles into care, organizing, political, academic, and NGO work;
- one named coordination space, **Espacio de Coordinación Grietas en el Muro**, has a plausible partial Escuelita lineage and remained publicly active from 2016 through at least October 2024;
- a named former student, Levi Gahman, supplies a small individual trace from attendance to later organizing and public knowledge work;
- major Zapatista-influenced organizations often offered as examples cannot be credited to the Escuelita because transnational Zapatismo and some of those projects existed before 2013; and
- this search did not locate a participant registry, longitudinal alumni cohort, new residential commune or autonomous federation with a documented Escuelita origin, or measured safety, child-wellbeing, autonomy, retention, and material outcomes.

So the seeds demonstrably germinated. The evidence does not yet show a transplanted forest.

## What counts as a sprout

The research uses a six-level lineage ladder:

| Level | Test | Current evidence |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Encounter | attendance or use of materials | strong; thousands participated and materials circulated |
| 2. Continued relation | alumni remain identifiable and act together | strong but uneven; documented in multiple regions from 2014 through 2016 |
| 3. Practice transfer | participants apply principles in later work | qualitative evidence; no representative denominator |
| 4. New organization | named post-Escuelita project with documented alumni role | one plausible mixed-lineage candidate and a few individual traces |
| 5. Durable communal institution | persistent shared-life or governance system | not located with a direct Escuelita causal chain |
| 6. Human outcomes | later safety, autonomy, wellbeing, viability, or retention measured | not located |

This ladder matters because “inspired people,” “created a network,” “formed a project,” and “built a successful community” are not synonyms.

## The Escuelita was designed to travel — but design is not outcome

The official 2013 record says the first community session expanded from 500 to 1,000 and then 1,500 places because of demand. It describes host families, guardians or Votanes, community teachers, videos, and textbooks, and says the EZLN was considering sending teachers elsewhere if invited. A second official count listed about 3,000 invitations, about 2,500 affirmative replies, 1,500 community students for August, and about 200 more at CIDECI.

That is unusually deliberate movement pedagogy. It supplies a substantial exposure population and an outward intention. It does not supply an alumni registry, baseline, adoption measure, comparison group, or later outcome panel.

## Sprout 1: alumni became an organizational identity — F-193

The immediate evidence is not merely that former students remembered the experience. Alumni identified themselves publicly and acted in groups.

- In February 2014, UNAM members, including Escuelita alumni, issued a solidarity statement tied explicitly to their recent stay with Zapatista support bases.
- In May 2014, ten August 2013 alumni in Italy signed a joint denunciation after the attack at La Realidad.
- In July 2014, alumni in Colima and Querétaro organized fundraising and coordination for Indigenous National Congress travel and the La Realidad gathering.
- In August 2015, organizations and Escuelita alumni in the Valley of Mexico called a march for the disappeared Ayotzinapa students.
- In 2016, Escuelita alumni were still named as organizers or a constituency for CompArte and Indigenous National Congress solidarity events.

The geographic spread and repeated public action establish levels 2 and part of 3: the school generated or reinforced a translocal alumni relation capable of mobilization. They do not reveal how many of the thousands of students remained active, whether the same groups persisted, or whether these actions created new shared-life institutions.

## Sprout 2: participants report carrying practices home — F-194

Charlotte María Sáenz organized five or six CIIS study trips between roughly 2012 and 2018. The trips included the Escuelita and other Zapatista encounters. In a 2023 public conversation, she reported later meeting former students at an Albuquerque Zapatista encuentro and seeing them carry principles into care work, organizing, political work, and NGO work.

Her 2023 and 2024 research reaches beyond that anecdote. Interviews with pro-Zapatista interlocutors involved in local change processes describe learning to listen differently, confronting internalized hierarchy and vanguard habits, recovering historical and ancestral memory, and organizing collectivities across nation-state boundaries.

This is meaningful level-3 evidence. It is also bundled exposure. The participants encountered Zapatismo over years through the Escuelita, other encounters, accompaniment, texts, and networks. The publications do not identify an Escuelita-only cohort, count failed adaptations, name a denominator, or measure later community outcomes.

## Sprout 3: Grietas en el Muro is the strongest durable candidate — F-195

An official 2016 event notice says that collectives and individuals who were adherents to the Sixth Declaration and Escuelita students decided to create an alternate Mexico City site for the Zapatista CompArte festival. The organizers included the **Espacio de Coordinación Grietas en el Muro**.

The coordination space then remains visible:

- in 2019, as a convener of a collective trip to a CNI assembly and of Mexico City political education events;
- in 2022, as a convener of the eighteenth Mictlán Rebelde political-cultural gathering; and
- in an October 2024 CNI solidarity statement, as a named Mexican signatory organization.

This is the best candidate found for a post-Escuelita organization-level sprout: an initiative organized with Escuelita alumni became a named coordination space and remained publicly active for at least eight years.

The limit is important. The 2016 record describes mixed founders—older collectives, Sixth Declaration adherents, and Escuelita alumni. It does not say the Escuelita alone created Grietas en el Muro, give a member list, show continuous membership, describe internal governance, or measure human outcomes. It is a durable political-cultural coordination institution, not evidence of a replicated residential Zapatista community.

## Sprout 4: one traceable individual pathway — F-196

A December 2014 account by Levi Gahman describes a small Okanagan Valley student solidarity mobilization for Ayotzinapa. Its author note identifies him as a former Escuelita student and an organizer with Radical Action with Migrants in Agriculture. A 2016 article on Zapatista food sovereignty thanks the Zapatistas for accepting him into their school and again records his organizing and editorial roles. He later published scholarly and public work on autonomy, mutual aid, social reproduction, and Zapatismo.

This supplies a rare named path from participant to subsequent organizing and knowledge transmission. It does not show that the Escuelita caused RAMA, that the organization adopted a particular Escuelita practice, or that later organizing produced a durable communal institution or measured outcome. It is a micro-trace, not a cohort result.

## What must be excluded from Escuelita-specific credit — F-197

The strongest attribution control is the history before 2013.

- Abigail Andrews documented activists “taking Zapatismo home” in research published in 2011. She found both reflexive changes and tensions, including rifts with some allies and resources redirected from Chiapas toward local problems.
- Kara Zugman Dellacioppa's 2009 ethnography traced Zapatista-inspired community organizations in Mexico City and Los Angeles. Its Casa del Pueblo case was already organizing undocumented immigrants in Los Angeles.
- Manuel Callahan described Universidad de la Tierra Califas in 2012 as a project between network and collective pedagogy and as “Zapatismo beyond Chiapas.”

These are real sprouts of Zapatismo, but they precede the Escuelita. They show that the 2013-2014 school entered an already living transnational ecology. Later projects may have been strengthened, redirected, or connected by the Escuelita, but resemblance and chronology alone cannot establish descent.

The correct image is often **grafting into existing root systems**, not planting in empty ground.

## What the bounded search did not find — F-198

Across official EZLN and CNI archives, alumni statements, named participant accounts, academic articles and dissertations, candidate-organization histories, and pre-2013 controls, this unit did not locate:

1. a public roster linking the thousands of Escuelita participants to later projects;
2. a longitudinal or comparative study of Escuelita alumni;
3. a counted rate of practice adoption, organization formation, failure, or abandonment;
4. a new residential community, commune, or autonomous governing federation whose founders document the Escuelita as a necessary origin;
5. sustained observation of such a descendant's governance, conflict, membership, child, care, or exit practices; or
6. later safety, child wellbeing, autonomy, voluntary retention, material viability, or relational-flourishing outcomes.

This is a bounded null, not a historical claim that no such descendant exists. The public record is biased toward statements, events, intellectual influence, and successful visible networks. Quiet local adaptations, failures, and projects using different names will be easy to miss.

## What this means for the worldwide-innovation thesis

The Escuelita strengthens the case that Zapatismo is one of the most important generators of worldwide communal innovation. It did something many durable communities do not: it made ordinary governance, women's participation, resistance, education, and daily autonomous life available for outsiders to encounter through families and collective teachers rather than only through leaders or doctrine.

But the strongest support is for a **movement school and network generator**, not yet for a **replication engine producing verified new communes**.

The claim should therefore be split:

1. **Did it spread political imagination and practices?** Yes, with qualitative and network evidence.
2. **Did alumni organize afterward?** Yes, in multiple places; at least one mixed-lineage coordination space remained visible for years.
3. **Did it create durable new communal governance systems?** Not demonstrated by the located evidence.
4. **Did those systems improve how people live and love?** Unmeasured.

That is not a trivial result. Political education that creates durable relations, changes organizers' practice, and supports collective action is itself a consequential form of movement success. It is simply not the same empirical claim as successful communal replication.

## The research instrument the movement still needs

A defensible follow-up would reconstruct a stratified alumni cohort by session and geography, then record:

`attendance -> prior affiliation -> stated learning -> attempted adaptation -> organization created or changed -> persistence -> governance practice -> failures and corrections -> later human outcomes`

The comparison should include preexisting Zapatista participants, new participants, nonattending applicants, projects that dissolved, and projects that stopped using Zapatista language. Without those controls, charismatic success stories will dominate the record and the Escuelita will receive credit for older movements or lose credit for adaptations that no longer display its name.

## Bottom line

Some seeds sprouted. The clearest growth is a dispersed political ecology: alumni groups, solidarity circuits, cultural-political coordination, changed practice inside existing work, and continuing Zapatista learning networks.

The research does not yet justify the stronger statement that the Escuelita generated a population of durable new communities with demonstrated human outcomes. For the owner's purposes, that missing lineage-and-outcomes study is now a precise research gap rather than a vague doubt.
