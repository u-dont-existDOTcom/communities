## Why there is no new gap

The Israeli kibbutz/moshav cooperative crisis supplies strong real-world evidence that broad mutual guarantees can transmit losses across otherwise autonomous cooperatives. That mechanism is important but not obscure: it is recognizable cooperative-finance/systemic-risk logic and was rejected by the hostile common-sense/nearest-neighbor gate as a new conceptual gap.

The correct use is to sharpen the existing federation architecture.

## G-027 evidence update — solidarity is not unlimited joint liability

Historical Israeli cooperative agriculture used multi-level mutual guarantees to substitute for conventional collateral and pool risk. Kibbutzim, moshavim, regional cooperatives, and movement-level institutions could be financially connected through credit and guarantee structures. The arrangements supported development and access to capital, but the 1980s crisis showed that they could also transmit liabilities across nodes after an asymmetric shock.

Academic and World Bank accounts describe a post-crisis movement away from the broad guarantee architecture. The exact sequence differed across cooperative layers and settlements; the robust conclusion is that cross-node liability was progressively constrained, and Rosenthal & Eiges' 2014 review identifies the 1996 second kibbutz settlement as dismantling the mutual-guarantee system.

This supplies a necessary boundary to G-027's non-transactional-support lesson:

> **Do not confuse solidarity with joint liability.**

Before a federation transfers resources, classify the instrument:

1. **Grant / direct aid** — no repayment claim.
2. **Pooled reserve / insurance-like protection** — defined covered events, contributions, reserve, eligibility, limits, claims, and appeal.
3. **Loan** — named borrower and repayment terms.
4. **Guarantee / surety** — explicit contingent liability if another node cannot pay.
5. **Shared-enterprise capital/fixed-cost commitment** — responsibility for collectively approved committed costs, with explicit exit/termination rules.
6. **Non-transactional exchange / training / relationship-building** — no bilateral debt unless separately agreed.

Operational controls:

- guarantee authority must be explicit and separately approved rather than implied by `mutual aid`;
- publish gross and contingent exposure by community/shared institution;
- cap/collateralize guarantees where appropriate;
- stress-test simultaneous guarantee calls against federation liquidity;
- never retroactively convert aid into debt;
- separate sunk/committed shared-enterprise costs from future use obligations;
- define exit/termination cost allocation before building a shared enterprise.

The FEC and kibbutz cases therefore identify opposite accounting errors:

- FEC's old LEX made some aid/connection too transactional by assuming parity;
- Israeli movement finance could make solidarity too financially coupled by turning cooperation into broad contingent liability.

The target is **purpose-specific accounting and liability**, not maximal reciprocity and not maximal pooling.

## G-024 evidence update — a service federation should not silently become a credit federation

The 1989 World Bank analysis of Israeli agricultural cooperatives describes second-order supply cooperatives that began with direct service functions but expanded into major financial intermediaries backed by mutual liability. It also describes control problems increasing with the size/organizational distance of those higher-order cooperatives, and cases where failing regional enterprises pulled supply cooperatives into financing their losses/debt service.

The crisis-reform architecture explicitly proposed that supply cooperatives return to input supply/product marketing and cease financial intermediation, while failing regional enterprises would be closed or sold and member cooperatives would deal more directly with banks.

This is direct empirical support for G-024's modularity rule.

For every function-specific federation institution, distinguish authority over:

- service operations;
- ownership/capital;
- ordinary accounts payable;
- lending/borrowing;
- guarantee/surety;
- pooled-risk claims;
- emergency grants.

A health pool, transport cooperative, purchasing service, archive, dispute body, or training network should not acquire movement-wide borrowing/guarantee powers merely because it already serves many communities. If it does need those powers, treat them as a separate risk-bearing function with explicit exposure limits, reserves, reporting, succession, and failure isolation.

## Shared-enterprise exit update

Rosenthal & Eiges also report that post-crisis regional enterprises needed rules preventing the fixed-cost share of a departing owner from simply being dumped onto remaining kibbutzim, which could induce additional exits and threaten the enterprise.

Do not generalize this into permanent exit debt. The narrower design rule is:

- identify costs already irreversibly committed on behalf of owners;
- specify each owner's responsibility for those commitments ex ante;
- distinguish them from future variable/use costs;
- define termination/transfer/buyout rules before investment;
- do not let either the leaver or the remaining nodes discover the liability only during crisis.

This belongs under G-024/G-027 rather than a separate gap.
