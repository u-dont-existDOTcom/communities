## Main result

Volume 43 adds two governance lessons: **protected dissent can stop a policy that intrudes on children's family bonds, while a norm against rules can itself become a rule that disables admission control, protection, and usable exit.**

1. **A child-separation policy can be stopped before implementation.** Krall reports that New Harmony proposed moving children aged fourteen and older from their homes into communal boarding houses. Sarah Pears objected both to loss of parental care and to the household labor consequences. Her letters also described exhaustion and complaints about women's labor that usually went unheard; her family left in 1826. Krall states that women's protests meant the child-removal plan was never implemented. The source does not reconstruct a formal vote, identify every protester, or isolate protest from other reasons the policy may have died. The transferable point is therefore procedural: preserve family contact as a non-waivable interest, notify affected adults and children, protect collective objection and credible exit, record the decision, and verify nonimplementation rather than assuming an announced policy became practice. (F-152)

2. **Rule-avoidance can disable self-protection.** Metcalf's review of Olive Jones's participant history reports that Graham Downs resisted membership and governance structure in the name of anarchism. The community then had little ability or willingness to refuse arrivals; the review describes some as avoiding work, abusing alcohol or drugs, running from law enforcement, or participating in violence. Founders reportedly felt used and unable to protect communal life. Because land and buildings belonged to the trust, some unhappy early members had nothing to sell and remained. The community dwindled to six residents, and a new trustee board led by Jones later took over with the stated aim of changing the laissez-faire culture; the review records no later result. The lesson is not that anarchism or homelessness predicts danger, nor that the review's labels are diagnoses. It is that any governance philosophy needs conduct-specific admission, protection, separation, property, and review procedures that remain operable when ordinary goodwill fails. (F-153)

## Source and interpretation cautions

- F-152 relies on Krall's historical synthesis and primary letters and newspapers. The article states that protests prevented implementation, but it does not reconstruct a formal proposal record, vote, decision maker, protest roster, or independent causal test. Inspect the Pears letters, New Harmony minutes, boarding plans, newspapers, family records, and other participant accounts before presenting the sequence as a formal policy precedent.
- F-153 is a review of Olive Jones's participant history. Inspect the book, Renaissance Community Trust deed, admission and trustee records, police and incident records, financial and property documents, accounts from residents and neighbors, and later board actions before naming conduct, assigning diagnoses or prevalence, or claiming that the takeover repaired the community.
- Brown's 80-percent school-persistence statement is an author report in a brief reflective article. Inspect the 1996 dissertation, program records, enrollment denominator, definitions, comparison group, incident and safety records, attrition, follow-up, and participant accounts before treating the program as effective for violent or persistently dangerous students.
- The Waco items review dramatizations and a documentary. They explicitly identify memoir bias, compression, and sensational framing and cannot substitute for primary records, hearings, litigation, participant accounts across positions, or child-protection evidence.
- Book reviews and book notes support their reviewers' direct summaries and evaluations until the reviewed books and underlying records are inspected. General theory and how-to guidance are design inputs rather than measured case outcomes.
- None of the sources supports a retrospective diagnosis of antisocial personality disorder or psychopathy. Findings describe attributed conduct, institutional structure, evidence limits, response processes, and outcomes.
