## Main result

Volume 40 adds three narrow lessons: **governance has to cross the boundary between household and business roles; nominally independent legal control is unsafe when the same fiduciary drafts and benefits from the instrument; and an outside civil or clinical process is not an independent safeguard when implicated leaders invoke it against dissent.**

1. **A participatory household needs an explicit constitution for its hierarchical business.** Vought reports that Kerista governed communal life cooperatively while Abacus used executive and supervisory roles. The same members could be lovers, supervisors, and executives; separating emotional issues into the home and performance issues into the workplace did not resolve those overlapping interests. When business pressure rose, the executive team laid off employees and, according to one participant, did not consult everyone about laying off commune members. The exclusion strained trust; a two-year orderly liquidation protected owners from legal and financial trouble, but a transaction-fee dispute added resentment and Kerista dissolved amid broader conflict. The source does not supply the governing instruments, meeting records, every affected member's account, or a causal test. It does show why a community-owned enterprise needs a domain-by-domain authority map, conflict disclosure and recusal, affected-member voice, records, appeal, and crisis rules before the same relationships carry incompatible powers. (F-143)

2. **A second trustee is not independent merely because adding the name signals humility.** Jones reports that Skaneateles adopted a collective deed after debate over whether legal ownership compromised its principles. Collins included lawyer Quincy A. Johnson as a co-trustee to avoid appearing selfish or dictatorial; the article says Johnson likely helped draft the deed and knew how to exploit its loopholes, while Collins's no-government and nonresistance commitments kept him from seeking state help to evict people who had outstayed their welcome. The author reports that the community lost half its land, paid roughly $3,000 to remove opponents, later failed to incorporate, and dissolved even though its property could cover its debts. The article does not adjudicate Johnson's conduct, reconstruct the complete title chain, or isolate the cause of dissolution. It nevertheless identifies a design failure worth testing: fiduciary independence requires conflict and beneficiary review, separate counsel, bounded powers, member-visible title records, and a precommitted lawful remedy—not a symbolic extra signature. (F-144)

3. **An external civil process can be used as an internal dissent sanction.** Sakmyster reports that John S. Hobart advocated a reform permitting “spiritual marriage” for some Shakers. The White Water elders and Central Ministry immediately sought to silence him and unsuccessfully tried to have a civil court declare him a “lunatic.” The article does not provide the petition, evidence, legal standard, hearing record, Hobart's account, or reason for failure. A prior ledger source, F-040, also preserves a separate report of dangerous intent attributed to Hobart without a response sequence. The new source therefore cannot prove that the petition was baseless. It does establish the conflict risk when leaders who oppose a member's doctrine initiate a competency or clinical pathway: the route needs conduct-specific evidence, qualified and conflict-disclosed assessment, notice, representation, proportionality, review, and a protected channel for doctrinal disagreement. (F-145)

## Source and interpretation cautions

- F-143 relies heavily on retrospective interviews with former Kerista members and the author's synthesis. Inspect Abacus and Kerista governing documents, ownership records, meeting minutes, layoff records, benefit terms, liquidation documents, and accounts from laid-off commune and outside employees before assigning responsibility or causal weight.
- F-144 combines a deed abstract and contemporary documents with later chroniclers whose language is strongly adverse to Johnson. Inspect the deed, mortgage, title chain, Johnson's account, court or eviction record, the reported payouts, incorporation petition, legislative record, and dissolution accounting before describing fraud, exact loss, or cause.
- F-145 reports an unsuccessful civil effort but does not supply the proceeding. Retrieve the cited 2014 article and underlying petition, evidence, court disposition, and participant records. Reconcile those materials with the dangerous-intent report already preserved in F-040; do not infer diagnosis or a knowingly false petition.
- The Bruderhof “rule of silence” is a movement-authored statement reproduced in an article about a magazine. Without an application, grievance record, or later outcome, it remains a verification lead rather than evidence that a specific report was suppressed.
- None of the sources supports a retrospective diagnosis of antisocial personality disorder or psychopathy. Findings describe reported conduct, institutional structure, evidence limits, response processes, and outcomes.
