## Main result

Volume 42 adds two child-specific lessons: **adult-consented openness cannot waive a child's physical privacy, and collective childrearing cannot turn a child into an unprotected substitute caregiver while severing ordinary family bonds and outside capacity.**

1. **Child safeguarding begins in the physical design.** Sargisson reports that Centrepoint removed doors from toilets and showers as part of an adult project of sexual and bodily openness. A former resident who spent part of her childhood there said that a man followed her into the shower, watched her, and began touching her as she approached puberty; she stopped washing because smelling bad kept him away. Sargisson says that the children were not considered in the design of the homes and domestic structures, and cites a 29-participant retrospective project identifying neglect that included the absence of secure bathrooms and sleeping quarters. The article also reports prosecutions and imprisonment for sexual and substance abuse, including abuse of minors, but it does not identify the offender in this account, reconstruct a case-specific internal response, or show that architecture alone caused the abuse. The transferable point is narrower: private bathing and sleeping space, child-controlled access boundaries, named adult supervision, confidential reporting, and independent advocacy are safeguards, not optional expressions of adult household preference. (F-149)

2. **A child cannot be the community's fallback care system.** Altus's review of Rachel Israel's memoir reports corporal punishment, rejected modern medicine, inadequate food and schooling, provision of marijuana and psychedelics, separation from her mother and siblings, and a lack of protective adult attention. By age twelve, Israel says she was expected to watch, feed, change, and launder for younger children. She left the Love Family at fifteen during a large exodus and describes culture shock, a relationship with her mother that never recovered, loss of an ordinary sibling relationship, and later depression and anxiety, while also identifying friendship, beauty, lessons, travel, meditation, courage, and other benefits. Altus warns that the self-published memoir contains serious factual and editing errors and represents one perspective, even though it incorporates diary excerpts and interviews. The finding therefore supports child-specific workload limits, a named accountable adult, protected family bonds, education and health rights, a right to refuse caregiving, and transition support; it does not establish group-wide prevalence, diagnose causation, or net positive memories against reported harm. (F-150)

## Source and interpretation cautions

- F-149 combines Sargisson's interview with one former Centrepoint child resident, the article's synthesis, cited retrospective studies, and a general report of prosecutions. Inspect interview records, the Gibson-Morgan-Woolley-Powis studies, Centrepoint plans and rules, participant accounts, police and court records, and child-protection records before identifying an offender, assigning prevalence, or stating a legal or causal conclusion.
- F-150 is based on Altus's review of a first-person memoir issued by a pay-to-publish press. The reviewer identifies diary excerpts and interviews but also serious factual and editorial errors. Inspect the memoir, diary provenance, interview records, accounts from other children and adults, Love Family rules, school and health records, and any official records before assigning group-wide prevalence, authority, motive, or causation.
- Positive childhood memories in the Love Family do not erase the reported deprivation, family separation, or later harm; neither do adverse reports establish that every experience or member was the same. The ledger preserves both propositions.
- The social-sustainability study is a small comparative snapshot during the COVID-19 period, uses opportunistic and snowball recruitment, and favors English speakers. Its relationships are hypotheses and structured observations, not causal estimates for all intentional communities.
- East Wind's management conflict was still unfolding when the researcher left. The article documents observed structures, statements, burnout, a resignation, and a meeting notice, but not the later resolution of the advertisement dispute.
- Book reviews support their reviewers' direct summaries and evaluations until the reviewed books and underlying records are inspected. Fictional communities are not factual case evidence.
- None of the sources supports a retrospective diagnosis of antisocial personality disorder or psychopathy. Findings describe reported conduct, institutional structure, evidence limits, response processes, and outcomes.
