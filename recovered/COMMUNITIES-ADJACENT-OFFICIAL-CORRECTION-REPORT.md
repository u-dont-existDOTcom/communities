The records are official, but an official endpoint is not automatically a safety outcome. License revocation establishes removal of licensed authority; it does not establish patient recovery or child wellbeing. A conviction establishes criminal liability for the offence adjudicated; it does not measure every allegation, survivor recovery, institutional reform, or risk in successor bodies.

## Source-level findings

### F-180 — Professional discipline can remove a therapist's authority without completing family repair

New York's Office of the Professions explains that it investigates and prosecutes professional misconduct in covered professions and that the Board of Regents makes final disciplinary dispositions. Two 1997 psychology entries independently verify part of the later correction reported at review level in F-156.

On 12 June 1997, the Board revoked the psychology license of Helen M. Fogarty, also identified in the entry as Helen Moses, and imposed a $15,000 fine. The public summary reports findings involving verbal abuse of a patient, multiple relationships, billing statements for services not provided, attempted undue influence on a patient to abandon misconduct accusations, and sexual relations with two supervisees.

On 18 July 1997, the Board revoked Marc Rice's psychology license. The public summary reports findings involving verbal abuse, direction to consume alcohol, direction against being alone with the patient's children, direction to end communication with the patient's parents, multiple relationships, and direction to maintain multiple sexual relationships.

These are conduct-specific official dispositions. They show why therapy, professional supervision, billing, complaint evidence, intimate relationships, and family contact cannot remain inside one authority chain. They also show a correction route that does not depend on the challenged therapist's consent or the community's internal view of the conduct.

The record has important limits. The official pages do not name the Sullivan Institute. The link to the Sullivanian sequence comes from the separately limited F-156 source lead. The pages do not reproduce the full charges, evidence, defenses, hearings, or any appeal. They do not adjudicate the Pappo custody dispute, verify discipline for every person named by the review, or establish that publicity caused the professional actions.

Most importantly, loss of a license is an authority outcome, not a child or patient outcome. The summaries report no restored family contact, treatment for harmed people, compensation, record correction, institution-wide remedy, or later wellbeing. A correction dashboard must retain those as separate fields.

### F-181 — An inquiry can expose a blocked reporting system while leaving implementation unfinished

The New Zealand Royal Commission's final report gives the Gloriavale lead an official system-level record. The Inquiry found that a policy operating from 1989 to 2002 prevented reporting crimes to secular agencies and limited contact with the outside world. It also found that leadership routinely failed to respond, or responded inadequately, to abuse reports. The report records that the founder handled such reports until the mid-1990s and was later convicted of sexual offending, including against young people.

The final report did not stop at historical narrative. It recorded a government function created in August 2022 to coordinate agencies dealing with Gloriavale and noted that the function ended on 31 December 2023. It summarized December 2023 government briefings that described continuing risks to child wellbeing and continuing Police and Oranga Tamariki responses. Because proceedings mentioned in those briefings may have changed, the ledger treats them only as dated facts in the 2024 report, not as a 2026 case-status register.

Recommendation 88 states the correction standard: government should take all practicable steps to ensure the ongoing safety of children, young people, and adults in care at Gloriavale. The surrounding recommendations call for independent monitoring bodies that can investigate complaints, conduct proactive and reactive visits, review provider responses, publish outcomes, share information, and refer matters to enforcement or regulatory bodies.

This record establishes that an internal ban on secular reporting is not a neutral cultural choice when the same authority controls allegations and care. The corrective functions must include direct external intake, preserved policy and screening records, survivor evidence, cross-agency information sharing, independent oversight, and continuing outcome review.

It does not establish exact prevalence, adjudicate every reported event, or show that Recommendation 88 was implemented. It supplies no complete later-outcome panel for children, survivors, families, accused people, or the institution. The end of a coordination function is not proof of failure, but neither can it be counted as successful transition while the same official record identifies continuing risks.

### F-182 — Disclosure, external investigation, conviction, support, and institutional repair are separate stages

IICSA's September 2021 statutory investigation report combines survivor testimony, police evidence, public-hearing material, prosecution outcomes, and sector analysis. Its Jesus Fellowship section records testimony that children who disclosed could be disbelieved or blamed while an accused person might be prayed over or moved. Other sections record testimony that institutional reputation and a cycle of forgiveness obstructed reporting and response.

In 2013, the Jesus Fellowship publicly invited disclosures. The disclosures were passed to Northamptonshire Police, which began Operation Lifeboat in 2014. The inquiry reports that seven suspects were tried for child sexual offences and six were convicted of offences relating to 11 children. It also recorded a further police investigation as ongoing at the time. Those numbers are kept distinct: a disclosure is not a charge, a person investigated is not a person convicted, and a conviction is not a finding about every institutional allegation.

The inquiry's wider conclusions clarify the institutional boundary. Internal investigatory or disciplinary processes must not substitute for reporting to external authorities. They cannot be the only adjudicative mechanism for abuse, supply justice or redress to a victim, or alone assess risk to others. The inquiry also distinguished pastoral support from qualified therapeutic services and called for victim-focused policies, whistleblowing provisions, professional child-protection advice, compulsory training, and external oversight.

The disclosure-to-police sequence demonstrates that an institution can eventually transmit information in a way that produces external adjudication. It does not show why particular people disclosed, whether every record reached police, whether survivors received adequate support, whether all risks were addressed, or whether successor bodies were safe. The report predates later redress and closure material; those later records were not silently treated as statutory-inquiry findings.

The inquiry quoted some witness descriptions of members' mental health, criminal history, and organisational character. Those descriptions are not promoted as diagnoses, screening variables, or safety proxies. The promoted mechanism is about accessible disclosure, evidence routing, independence, adjudication, support, and follow-up.

## Independence, preservation, and recusal tests

### Independence

Independence is functional, not ceremonial. The reviewer must be outside the authority chain implicated by the report and must be able to obtain records, hear affected people privately, refer matters onward, and publish or otherwise preserve a correction.

D-001 had a regulator capable of ending licensed authority. D-002 used an inquiry and multiple state bodies after internal rules had restricted outside reporting. D-003 moved disclosures to police and courts and later exposed the process to a statutory inquiry. None supports an internal council simply renaming itself independent.

### Evidence preservation

Each record shows why a community needs a minimal preservation route before facts are tested:

1. record the observable conduct, disclosure, date, recipient, and immediate safety action;
2. preserve originals and access logs outside the challenged leader's exclusive control;
3. distinguish allegation, corroboration, administrative finding, charge, conviction, and later outcome;
4. retain reasons for screening, referral, non-referral, recusal, delay, and closure;
5. protect confidential reporting and prevent retaliation or family-contact leverage; and
6. give lawful authorities the information necessary for their own process without conducting a parallel coercive interrogation.

The records do not justify compelled confession, public accusation, amateur forensic interviewing, or broad circulation of sensitive information.

### Recusal

Recusal is required when a person controls both the relationship and the evidence: therapist and patient; supervisor and supervisee; leader and child; caregiver and reporter; institutional reputation manager and investigator; or membership authority and survivor-support route.

A recused person must not select the interviewer, edit the record, determine credibility, control family contact, impose the interim condition, decide referral, or hear the appeal. D-001 shows that professional review must sit outside the therapist's chain. D-002 and D-003 show that spiritual authority, forgiveness, relocation, and reputation management cannot replace safeguarding assessment or legal referral.

## Family contact and survivor support

Family contact is both a possible evidence source and a possible lever of control. D-001 includes official findings about therapist direction affecting contact with children and parents. D-002 records barriers to leaving and losses of family and assets in the inquiry's broader Gloriavale evidence. D-003 records how distrust of outside bodies and fear of institutional consequences could inhibit disclosure.

The transfer rule is not unrestricted contact in every circumstance. It is that any safety-related limitation must have lawful authority, a conduct-specific basis, independent review, a defined duration, a child-centered plan, and appeal. A challenged therapist, leader, or community body cannot manufacture a family-separation narrative and then control its evidence.

Survivor support is also distinct from investigation. A support person should not promise a legal outcome, test credibility, pressure forgiveness, demand public disclosure, or make housing, membership, benefits, or family contact contingent on cooperation. Support must remain available when a report is unproven, a prosecution fails, a person declines to participate, or an inquiry ends.

## Official-correction outcome rule

For every correction record, retain separate fields for:

1. initial disclosure or observable conduct;
2. immediate protection and its legal authority;
3. evidence custodian and access history;
4. challenged roles and recusals;
5. external referral, recipient, date, and screening result;
6. administrative, licensing, civil, criminal, or inquiry process;
7. exact finding or non-finding and standard applied;
8. sanction, restriction, remedy, support, or recommendation;
9. restored or lawfully limited family contact;
10. survivor and child wellbeing over time;
11. alleged or adjudicated actor's later access to children or authority;
12. institution and successor-body implementation;
13. recurrence, complaints, and unreported-risk indicators; and
14. fixed review dates, responsible external body, and public or confidential completion record.

These fields must not be reduced to a single success variable. Removal of authority, institutional dissolution, punishment, compensation, survivor recovery, child safety, and prevention of recurrence can move in different directions.

## Transfer boundary

Potentially transferable design features are narrow:

- a confidential, child-accessible route to an outside safeguarding body;
- ordinary preservation of observable facts and original records;
- mandatory recusal within the private organisation;
- immediate noncoercive safety measures available to any ordinary resident under law;
- prompt contact with qualified or emergency services;
- firewalls between therapy, spiritual care, family contact, membership, housing, work, benefits, evidence, and discipline;
- survivor support separate from credibility testing and adjudication; and
- an implementation dashboard that continues after a license, conviction, closure, settlement, or report.

This evidence does not authorize residents to investigate crimes, conduct forensic interviews, diagnose anyone, restrain or seclude a child, remove a child, impose custody, revoke a professional license, compel treatment or confession, publish identities, retaliate, punish, banish, or substitute an internal process for lawful authorities.

## Gap consequences without prose change

Unit D strengthens six existing gaps without changing their B, C, or D classifications:

- **G-003 — protected reporting and evidence channels:** the route must bypass the challenged therapist or leader, preserve the distinction among allegation, finding, charge, and conviction, and remain available despite reputation or forgiveness pressure.
- **G-004 — practical founder and gatekeeper control:** control of therapy, supervision, complaints, family contact, and safeguarding records belongs in the capture audit.
- **G-005 — non-waivable rights:** support, lawful family contact, independent advice, and outside reporting do not depend on internal absolution, loyalty, or case success.
- **G-006 — outcome dashboard:** license loss, inquiry publication, conviction, closure, redress, child safety, survivor wellbeing, family repair, recurrence, and successor compliance are separate outcomes.
- **G-009 — collective childrearing:** shared adult care never creates authority to suppress a child's disclosure, relocate an accused person internally as the only response, or adjudicate abuse through spiritual discipline.
- **G-013 — external couplings:** professional regulators, police, courts, child-protection bodies, survivor services, and independent monitors perform different functions and need named triggers, records, and follow-up.

Gap counts remain 8 B, 7 C, and 3 D. No article prose was drafted or revised.
