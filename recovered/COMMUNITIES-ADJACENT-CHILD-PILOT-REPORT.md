## Main result

The pilot does not supply the requested complete individual sequence for a persistently dangerous child. It does, however, replace the earlier undifferentiated null with three tested contrasts:

1. a dense family-style residential program can improve measured conduct during residence without showing a significant advantage one year after exit;
2. changing the whole residential setting and caregiver practice can reduce several categories of serious incident, while leaving other categories inconsistent and post-exit outcomes unknown; and
3. a structured child-plus-caregiver program can outperform standard community services through repeated follow-up, but the intervention includes trained providers and continuing individualized services rather than peer therapy alone.

The common design implication is bounded. A community can shape daily conditions, caregiver behavior, relationships, and opportunities to practice skills. That does not make cohesion, residence, or peer care a diagnosis, a validated safety screen, or durable treatment. Individual assessment, trained support, measurement by conduct category, review, family involvement, victim protection, transition planning, and later outcome checks remain separate functions.

## F-169 — family-style residential gains did not establish durable post-exit change

Kirigin, Braukmann, Atwater, and Wolf evaluated the original Achievement Place program, 12 Teaching-Family replications, and nine conventional Kansas group homes. The programs generally housed six to eight court-adjudicated youths. Selection of homes was not random: nearby Teaching-Family homes were asked to participate, and state agencies chose comparison homes they considered representative. The study used court and police records for alleged criminal and noncriminal offenses and institutionalization, plus ratings from youths, parents, teachers, boards, court personnel, and welfare personnel.

During treatment, Teaching-Family programs performed better on the rate of alleged criminal offenses, the proportion of youths involved in those offenses, and consumer ratings. They did not show significant during-treatment differences on noncriminal events such as running away, truancy, and curfew violations. In the year after treatment, none of the group differences was statistically significant on the reported outcomes. Youth and teacher ratings correlated with lower during-treatment criminal-offense ratios, but ratings did not create a durable outcome by themselves.

What this establishes:

- a small, family-style, community-based residential structure can be evaluated with conduct records and resident feedback rather than reputation alone;
- trained caregivers, explicit teaching, self-government, reinforcement, and quality-control structures were associated with better measured criminal-conduct outcomes during residence in this nonrandom comparison; and
- post-exit measurement can reverse an overly broad success claim based only on behavior inside the setting.

What it does not establish:

- random assignment, equivalent unmeasured risk, complete or consistently maintained court records, causal effect of any single program component, victim outcomes, or a persistently dangerous-child subgroup;
- a reliable effect on running away or other noncriminal conduct;
- a significant post-treatment advantage; or
- that an intentional community may assume juvenile-court, placement, or treatment authority.

Transfer limit: this is adjacent residential/group-home evidence. Its strongest transferable lesson is the need to distinguish behavior while surrounded by program structure from behavior after structure and support recede. It does not validate “long inner work” or residence as a safety screen.

Primary record: [Kirigin et al. (1982), DOI 10.1901/jaba.1982.15-1](https://doi.org/10.1901/jaba.1982.15-1).

## F-170 — a setting-level residential intervention reduced some incidents, not every harm category

Izzo and colleagues studied CARE—Children and Residential Experiences—across 11 group-care agencies serving mostly children referred from child welfare. Agencies averaged about 24 residents and 13 residential staff at implementation start; most served girls and boys aged roughly 7 to 18. Caregivers lived in the home for one- or two-week shifts. Before CARE, the agencies used local rules, consequences, and activities but lacked a coherent evidence-based childcare model.

CARE organized practice around six principles: relationship-based, trauma-informed, developmentally focused, family-involved, competence-centered, and ecologically oriented care. Each agency created an implementation team including leaders, supervisors, and training or clinical staff. Leaders and teams received a five-day manualized training; local trainers prepared other staff; consultants returned quarterly for observation, feedback, coaching, reflective-practice routines, and work on organizational barriers.

The study compared monthly incident trends during a 12-month baseline with 36 months of implementation using mixed-effects count models. Relative to baseline trends, implementation was associated with significant reductions in aggression toward staff, property destruction, and running away. Results for aggression toward peers and self-harm were less consistent across cohorts. The design was a staggered interrupted time series rather than randomized assignment. Some baseline rates were increasing; historical change or agency-specific conditions could explain part of the result. The researchers also disclosed that the program developer directed the project and that most authors worked on the CARE research and implementation team, although they reported no direct implementation revenue.

What this establishes:

- serious incidents are partly setting events: staff expectations, relationships, emotional response, routines, and the physical/social environment can affect escalation;
- a whole-setting change needs an implementation team, leadership participation, training, coaching, observation, and repeated incident measurement—not a slogan about community healing; and
- incident categories should be reported separately because an apparent overall improvement can conceal weak or inconsistent results for peer aggression or self-harm.

What it does not establish:

- individual allegation, assessment, treatment plan, review, recurrence, victim recovery, or post-exit wellbeing;
- random causal attribution;
- that fewer incident reports always mean fewer incidents rather than changed detection, thresholds, or reporting;
- effectiveness in a lay-run intentional community; or
- that relationship quality may replace independent safeguarding, clinical care, or lawful emergency action.

Transfer limit: this is adjacent residential/group-care evidence. It supports a measured, developmentally informed setting function while strengthening the firewall around individual clinical assessment and independent review.

Primary record: [Izzo et al. (2016), DOI 10.1007/s11121-016-0649-0](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11121-016-0649-0).

## F-171 — structured child-plus-caregiver treatment outperformed standard services, with continuing support

Burke and Loeber independently evaluated SNAP—Stop Now and Plan—in an intent-to-treat randomized trial. The sample comprised 252 boys aged 6 to 11 whose aggressive, rule-breaking, or antisocial behavior exceeded clinical criteria. Families were assigned to SNAP (130 boys) or standard community services (122 boys). SNAP began with a three-month group component and could continue with individualized services through the 15-month study. Outcomes were measured at baseline and 3, 9, and 15 months.

SNAP combined child skill development with caregiver participation. The study reported greater reductions in aggression, conduct problems, overall externalizing behavior, and several symptom counts than standard services. The adjusted treatment difference for the primary parent-rated aggression outcome was statistically significant and small (Cohen's d about 0.29). Some benefits remained at the one-year follow-up, while others did not. Among the 150 participants old enough for juvenile jurisdiction by study end, 22.4 percent in standard services and 12.2 percent in SNAP had any new charges; that difference did not reach conventional significance. The number of charges was significantly higher in standard services, but event counts were small.

The program appeared more effective for boys with higher initial severity, but this is a treatment-response result—not a personality or danger-screen validation. Important limits include parent-reported primary outcomes, an all-boy sample, recruitment selection, unequal service uptake, additional services in both conditions, and the fact that individualized SNAP intervention continued during follow-up. The 15-month result therefore is not a clean test of durability after treatment stops.

What this establishes:

- a conduct-specific, developmentally targeted intervention can combine child problem-solving and emotion-regulation work with caregiver participation and repeated outcome review;
- randomized assignment can distinguish program effects from ordinary community services more credibly than an uncontrolled success story; and
- outcome reporting should separate symptom/behavior scales, any justice contact, number of charges, service use, severity, and maintenance over time.

What it does not establish:

- a residential or intentional-community process;
- a full individual victim-safety and allegation-adjudication sequence;
- effectiveness for girls, adolescents, every form of serious violence, or children who remain dangerous after treatment;
- durable outcomes after all services cease; or
- authority for peers or community leaders to diagnose, compel disclosure, or conduct treatment.

Transfer limit: this is adjacent clinical/community evidence. It supports early independent assessment, child skills, caregiver training, individualized follow-on, fidelity review, and repeated outcomes. It challenges any claim that communal immersion by itself is an adequate response.

Primary record: [Burke and Loeber (2015), DOI 10.1007/s11121-014-0490-2](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11121-014-0490-2).

## Gap reconciliation

The 18-gap architecture remains sufficient: eight B, seven C, and three D.

- G-002: F-169 through F-171 constrain “the community is the therapist.” Setting and relationships can matter, but trained intervention, care firewalls, measurement, and transition remain necessary.
- G-005: F-171 strengthens the need for independent child assessment, caregiver involvement, and a clinical route outside membership or discipline.
- G-006: F-169 makes during-residence and post-exit outcomes separate dashboard fields; F-170 requires conduct-category reporting; F-171 requires service exposure and continuing-treatment status.
- G-009: F-170 and F-171 add developmentally focused caregiving, family involvement, separate peer-aggression measurement, and repeated child outcomes without authorizing collective discipline.
- G-018: none of the sources validates personality, productivity, commitment, therapeutic fluency, or long residence as a safety screen. The pilot instead uses conduct records, structured measures, comparison conditions, and follow-up.
