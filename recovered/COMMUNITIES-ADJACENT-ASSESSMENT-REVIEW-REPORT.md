## Source-level findings

### F-172 — Safety response is a sequence of different functions

NICE NG10 remains the published guidance as of 15 August 2026, but NICE decided in 2024 to update it fully and expects the replacement on 28 January 2027. The provisional update materials are not final guidance. This time-sensitive status is part of the finding rather than a footnote.

For children and young people, the current recommendations join several elements that must not be collapsed into one community judgment:

- developmentally specific staff training;
- safeguarding and child involvement;
- collaboration with people holding parental responsibility;
- assessment of underlying needs, conduct history, triggers, trauma, prior responses, communication, language, cognitive, and cultural factors;
- de-escalation and an opportunity to move away before restriction;
- restrictive intervention only after attempts to defuse the situation fail;
- continuous physical and emotional monitoring during restriction;
- a prohibition on using parent contact, social interaction, food, fluids, or corporal punishment to force compliance;
- senior approval and early multidisciplinary review for seclusion;
- immediate post-incident debrief after harm is contained;
- a separate, service-user-led external review with staff from outside the ward, as soon as possible and no later than 72 hours; and
- reports, plan correction, outcome monitoring, and governing-body oversight.

This is an authoritative process boundary, not evidence that every component independently reduces violence. Its professional, organizational, and statutory powers do not transfer to a private community.

### F-173 — SAVRY distinguished short-term institutional-violence groups, not individuals with certainty

Gammelgård and colleagues studied 208 adolescents in residential psychiatry, correctional schools, or forensic units. SAVRY information came from medical and child-welfare records; institutional violence was taken from records for the following six months or until earlier discharge. Adjusted for sex, age, diagnosis, service level, and follow-up time, the reported relative odds of institutional violence were 27.85 in the high summary-risk group and 3.83 in the moderate group compared with the low group.

That is evidence of group discrimination in the studied settings. It is not an individual probability, a complete false-positive and false-negative account, an intervention evaluation, a later community outcome, or permission for an intentional community to administer its own test. A high relative odds ratio may still coexist with a low positive predictive value when an outcome is uncommon.

Access limit: the publisher's primary abstract and bibliographic records supplied the promoted facts. The complete paper and licensed manual would need inspection before professional application. No proprietary instrument items are reproduced here.

### F-174 — START:AV field performance was partial and history-heavy

In a 98-bed Dutch medium- and high-secure civil youth-care service, 13 trained treatment coordinators completed 106 final-version Dutch START:AV assessments. A blinded research assistant then coded 3,065 incidents from 21,572 progress notes and 106 treatment reports over four months.

Final risk judgments significantly discriminated physical aggression, institutional violations, substance use, self-injury, and victimization. They did not significantly discriminate nonviolent offenses, unauthorized leave, or health neglect. Total scores had limited outcome validity and poor interrater reliability in the 30-case reliability sample. For the outcomes with enough events for testing, final judgments added no significant predictive value over lifetime history.

The source therefore establishes an assessment limit as well as a capability. The instrument is outcome-specific, information-dependent, time-limited, and meant for trained professional judgment with frequent reassessment. The study did not show that using it reduced incidents, improved wellbeing or victim safety, or decreased liberty-restricting measures. The authors explicitly identify that management-effect question as future research.

The first author worked for the institution that publishes and trains on the Dutch guide, and three authors translated it. They reported no royalties and no study funding. That disclosure is preserved without treating it as disqualification.

### F-175 — Helpful debriefing did not reduce event rate or severity

One Canadian centre implemented staff-requested TALK debriefs after patient behavioral events in its acute child and adolescent mental-health units. TALK moves through a target, analysis of what went well and what could improve, learning points, actions, and delegated follow-up. The project separately tracked monthly event rate and the share of events that caused or nearly caused harm.

Neither event rate nor severity decreased significantly after implementation. Informal staff feedback nevertheless described emotional support, more open team conversation, learning about vicarious trauma, identification of privacy and least-restrictive-care issues, and a route for patient concerns to enter updated care plans.

Those are different outcomes. The source does not show that debriefing prevents incidents, and it does not evaluate independent review. Patients were not involved in the project or debriefs, feedback was informal and unquantified, use was inconsistent, and workload surges reduced access. This negative safety result is valuable because it blocks a common inference: a supportive internal review is not automatically prevention, independent investigation, or completed correction.

## Boundary map

This map separates functions. It is not medical, legal, or operational instruction for an emergency.

### Observable and preservable by ordinary residents

Within ordinary law and without making a diagnosis, residents can preserve conduct-specific information:

- what was seen or heard, by whom, when, and where;
- the immediate sequence, apparent target, injuries, damage, threats, access to weapons, and current location;
- contemporaneous words rather than personality labels;
- contextual triggers and attempted de-escalation;
- the child's and affected people's accounts, kept distinct;
- records, messages, video, medical information, and witness identities without editing them into a consensus narrative; and
- whether immediate protection or emergency help was sought.

Residents can use ordinary noncoercive safety actions available to anyone, such as creating distance, helping people leave danger, preserving evidence, and contacting qualified emergency or safeguarding services. The evidence does not authorize residents to restrain, seclude, medicate, interrogate, diagnose, or determine guilt.

### Requires independent qualified assessment

The studies and guideline place these functions outside a lay membership process:

- developmental, mental-health, communication, trauma, cognitive, cultural, and family assessment;
- structured professional judgment using multiple information sources;
- outcome-specific estimates that state uncertainty, review interval, and information limits;
- differentiation of risk to others, self-injury, victimization, absconding, substance use, health neglect, and rule violations;
- selection and monitoring of clinical intervention;
- assessment of physical and psychological contraindications to restrictive measures; and
- audit of reliability, false positives, false negatives, subgroup performance, and whether assessment improves management outcomes.

These tasks require the qualifications, information access, supervision, accountability, and legal framework of the relevant jurisdiction. Neither the name of an instrument nor a score makes a community member qualified.

### Requires lawful emergency or statutory authority

Emergency response, child protection, involuntary treatment, restriction of liberty, search by force, seclusion, medication, custody, and formal evidence adjudication are not powers that private membership creates. The current guidance's least-restrictive and review protections presuppose trained staff, resuscitation capacity, multidisciplinary authority, advocacy, records, external review, and governing oversight.

When a private community is itself implicated, internal debrief is especially insufficient. The process needs a route to an outside safeguarding, clinical, legal, regulatory, or emergency body with authority independent of membership leadership.

## False-positive, false-negative, and implementation limits

The unit does not support a danger cutoff. Its reusable measurement rules are narrower:

1. Report discrimination, calibration, base rate, sensitivity, specificity, positive and negative predictive values, and consequences of error where available; an odds ratio or AUC alone is incomplete.
2. Report outcome categories separately. Physical aggression, self-injury, victimization, substance use, leaving without authorization, and institutional rule violations are not one interchangeable endpoint.
3. Report who rated, what information they had, their training, agreement between raters, missing data, exclusions, and the reassessment interval.
4. Separate prediction from management effect. A tool can predict an outcome without its use reducing that outcome.
5. Separate immediate debrief, staff support, plan revision, independent investigation, action completion, and later safety.
6. Include the child's direct account and affected people's outcomes. Staff consensus is not a substitute.
7. Do not use poverty, diagnosis, disability, criminal history, nonconformity, productivity, therapeutic fluency, residence duration, or a high category by itself as proof of present danger.
