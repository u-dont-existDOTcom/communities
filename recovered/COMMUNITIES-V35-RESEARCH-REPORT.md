## Main result

Volume 35 adds two narrow design lessons from the Seventh Elect Church: **courts supply claim-specific remedies rather than a complete institutional safety audit, and an outside professional can become the asset-capture point unless the professional is also governed.**

1. **Map the question each external forum can actually answer.** Hainley reports that a 1925 court ordered Daniel Salwt to return property transferred by a former member who expected his healing, finding legal fraud while also finding that he sincerely believed his own claim. After Salwt's death, different proceedings treated title, a signed surrender contract, dissolution, alleged misconduct, and religious freedom as different questions. The Washington Supreme Court upheld church title and the former members' forfeiture of property under the contract; a later dissolution suit was dismissed after the judge excluded sexual testimony as unrelated to church doctrine. These outcomes do not establish that every judgment was correct, that the former members' allegations were true, or that present law would produce the same results. They show that a victory or loss on one pleaded transaction, title, contract, or constitutional question does not independently review threats, deprivation, leader conduct, member equity, or ongoing safety. Each concern needs its own preserved evidence, standing, forum, remedy, and follow-up. (F-119)

2. **“Outside” is not synonymous with independent.** Decades later, four elderly remaining members sued business manager Gerald Rogers for an accounting. Hainley reports that the court found Rogers had obtained their trust and control of church assets through false claims of shared interest and financial loyalty, diverted assets, and was ordered to make nearly $2 million in restitution. The reported judgment was upheld, but recovery of some property took almost ten years while Rogers tried to shield assets. The article does not reconstruct the original delegation, signatory controls, statements, audit access, or first warning. The transferable point is operational: an outside manager, director, lawyer, accountant, or trustee needs bounded authority, dual authorization, direct statements to the governed body, conflict disclosure, independent review, rapid revocation, and an escalation path. (F-120)

## Source and interpretation cautions

- F-119 is a historical legal-scope finding, not current legal advice. The 1925 decree, 1931 superior-court record, 1932 Washington Supreme Court opinion, 1934 pleadings and judgment, party evidence, and present law require independent review before any doctrinal proposition is reused.
- The former members' testimony about threats, confinement, food, sexual relations, and property is attributed allegation evidence. The article reports contrary continuing-member accounts and says no evidence showed underage involvement with Salwt or personal diversion of assets by him.
- F-120 relies on Hainley's synthesis of court conclusions and contemporary press. The accounting action, appellate record, delegation documents, asset-recovery record, church statements, and the exact amount recovered require inspection before the case is used as a financial-control precedent.
- Strezewski directly documents construction, account termination, consolidation, and later restoration, but he labels the route from dissatisfaction to relocation as inference and lists several alternative reasons for the move. The report therefore does not promote that causal theory.
- The Oneida article combines institutional publications, hostile outsider accounts, seceder narratives, diaries, memoirs, and later scholarship. It often marks uncertainty, including the limits of historical mental-health labels. Its corroboration does not convert retrospective or partisan claims into adjudicated facts.
- Short book reviews identify leads and contrasts, but their compression generally cannot establish a complete allegation, assessment, intervention, review, and later outcome.
