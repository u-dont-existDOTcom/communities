## Completion boundary

Unit C is complete at its four-study stop rule:

1. one family- and community-based randomized trial followed through middle adulthood;
2. one current family-treatment effectiveness trial with national-registry outcomes and an active usual-care comparator;
3. one randomized treatment-foster-care versus group-care trial with a 24-month outcome; and
4. one five-site residential-treatment trial with outcomes indexed to 12 and 24 months after release.

Four materially distinct findings were promoted as F-176 through F-179. The family/community and residential/group-care lanes remain separate. The unit contains two favorable and two null comparative results. Null results are promoted because they prevent treatment names, in-program improvement, completion, and transition planning from being counted as durable safety outcomes without comparative follow-up.

None of the four studies is intentional-community evidence. All depend on trained providers, public child-welfare or juvenile-justice systems, lawful placement or referral, research assessment, and external records. No clinical, custody, correctional, or treatment authority transfers to a private community.

The unit still does not supply the requested individual case sequence:

`persistent dangerous conduct -> conduct-specific allegation -> independent assessment -> intervention -> independent review -> post-intervention and later-life outcome`

These are group-level comparative studies. Even the trials involving serious or repeated violence do not reconstruct an individual allegation, victim response, adjudication, treatment review, and later outcome in one public case history.

## Source-level findings

### F-176 — One MST trial shows exceptional durability, not a universal family-treatment effect

Sawyer and Borduin followed 176 people who had been randomized during adolescence to multisystemic therapy (MST) or individual therapy. At intake, participants had averaged 3.9 felony arrests and nearly half had at least one violent-crime arrest. Official criminal and civil records were examined a mean 21.9 years after treatment, when participants averaged 37.3 years old.

The intent-to-treat results favored MST. Felony recidivism was 34.8% after MST and 54.8% after individual therapy. The reported frequency of misdemeanor offending was 5.0 times lower after MST, and the odds of a family-related civil suit were twice as high in the individual-therapy condition.

This is unusually long follow-up and plainly extends beyond active treatment. It establishes that a brief family- and community-focused intervention can be associated with materially different official outcomes in middle adulthood in this trial. It does not show that every multisystemic or family intervention works, that the same result transfers across jurisdictions and service systems, or which component caused the difference.

The endpoint is also narrow. Arrest, incarceration, and civil-court records do not measure unreported violence, victim safety, participant wellbeing, family burden, education, work, relationships outside court, or the support received during the intervening two decades. Long duration strengthens the durability test while enlarging the space for unmeasured later experiences.

The source is developer-led. Charles Borduin disclosed that he was a board member of MST Associates LLC, an organization providing MST training for youths with problem sexual behavior. The disclosure is preserved as a limit, not treated as automatic disqualification.

### F-177 — Current Norwegian FFT evidence does not support presumed superiority over active care

The 2026 Norwegian study is a useful counterweight because it tests a named family model in ordinary child-welfare services with a strong, heterogeneous comparator. The original trial enrolled 161 youths and families; 129 supplied usable national identifiers for the registry analysis. FFT generally involved eight to 30 sessions over three to six months.

The sample differed materially from the serious repeat-offender MST sample. Youths were referred for disruptive, aggressive, violent, school, or out-of-home-placement risk, but only seven were primarily referred for crime and only two had a prior arrest. The outcomes therefore concern prevention of first charges and placement as much as recidivism.

Treatment as usual was active. In the linked control group, 40.7% received MST, 27.1% parent training, and others family counselling or specialist services. The control group received more treatment hours on average, largely because of MST. The protocol and available control services also changed during recruitment so that families in crisis could enter.

National registers showed no statistically significant FFT-versus-TAU difference in criminal charges at 18 or 36 months or out-of-home placement at 18 months. This is not proof of exact equivalence. Recruitment stopped below target, missing identifiers produced a selected registry subsample, events were limited, and the achieved sample was powered only for relatively large effects. Point estimates favored FFT, but confidence intervals were wide and included no effect and, for some endpoints, ranges compatible with meaningful benefit and harm.

The durable finding is therefore methodological as well as substantive: active comparator strength, service dose, population severity, protocol deviations, linkage exclusions, and power must be reported before a treatment label is credited with long-term prevention.

The study was funded by the government-funded Norwegian Center for Child Behavioral Development. Two authors were employed by that organization, which implemented FFT in Norway from 2007 to 2024. The authors disclosed that relationship.

### F-178 — Treatment foster care outperformed group care at 24 months in one small trial

Juvenile-court judges referred girls aged 13 to 17 with serious and chronic delinquency. The 81 enrolled girls averaged more than 11 criminal referrals and were randomized to Multidimensional Treatment Foster Care (MTFC; 37) or one of 19 community group-care programs (44). The mean stay in the randomized placement was 174 days, making most of the 24-month endpoint a period beyond that placement.

MTFC was not simply a household form. State-certified foster parents received weekday case-monitoring calls and weekly supervision and support. The program used an individualized daily point-and-level process, individual therapy, family-of-origin therapy, close school monitoring, on-call support, psychiatric consultation, and fidelity review. Group-care programs varied in size, staffing, schooling, and treatment philosophy.

At 24 months, assignment to MTFC predicted a greater reduction in a latent delinquency construct made from criminal referrals, days in locked settings, and self-reported delinquency, controlling for baseline and age. The standardized coefficient was -0.36 and Cohen's d was 0.65. In raw means, MTFC participants spent 104.82 fewer days in locked settings across the two years.

The result supports a bundled, trained, supervised alternative to ordinary group care in this sample. It does not isolate foster-home scale, peer exposure, caregiver training, family therapy, public-school attendance, monitoring, or post-placement services. Nor does it measure victim safety, family burden, placement coercion, or adulthood outcomes. The study was small, mostly White, regional, and conducted by a team associated with model development; the authors themselves called for replication.

The transferable lesson is not “a family home heals.” It is that caregiver preparation, external supervision, family and school coordination, implementation monitoring, comparison against actual group care, and post-placement outcomes must travel together before a family-style setting receives causal credit.

### F-179 — In-residence improvement and relapse planning did not yield added post-release benefit

The Swedish trial recruited 81 males aged 16 to 21 who had been convicted of serious violent offenses and were living in five residential treatment homes. Participants were randomized across sites to group-based treatment as usual alone or the same care plus a manualized individual CBT module. The module began four to six months before release, aimed for 15 to 20 weekly sessions, and included problem-solving, cognitive self-control, relapse prevention, and identification of people who might provide prosocial support after release.

Both groups reported several pre-to-post improvements during residential treatment. Those improvements did not establish an added effect. Only 15 of the 38 people assigned to iCBT completed at least 15 sessions. Intent-to-treat analyses found no significant added effect on aggression at 12 months or nationwide registered reconviction at 12 and 24 months after release. Violent reconviction was 34% after iCBT plus TAU versus 23% after TAU at 12 months, and 50% versus 40% at 24 months. The differences were imprecise and not statistically significant.

Completer comparisons appeared somewhat more favorable but were no longer randomized: completers differed from noncompleters on several baseline characteristics. The trial was underpowered, treatment integrity and integration varied, and treatment as usual was active. Those limits prevent a claim that all individual CBT or continuing care is ineffective. They do not permit a favorable claim for this module either.

Most important for transition design, identifying prospective supports in a relapse plan is not the same as delivering and measuring aftercare. The source did not document a standardized post-release service that continued caregiver, school, vocational, psychiatric, or social support. Release-indexed follow-up exposed that gap.

The first author designed the iCBT intervention but reported no royalties, training, consulting, commercial, or financial gain. The sample also included legal adults, which limits transfer to a child-specific branch.

## Cross-study answers

### Do gains persist after dense treatment or staff support ends?

The answer is intervention-, comparator-, setting-, and outcome-specific.

- MST participants in one developer-led trial had better official criminal and civil outcomes more than two decades after treatment.
- FFT did not show statistically significant superiority over active ordinary services at 18 or 36 months in a lower-severity Norwegian child-welfare population.
- MTFC participants had better multi-method delinquency outcomes 24 months after baseline, well beyond the average randomized placement, than participants assigned to group care.
- A brief iCBT add-on did not improve aggression or reconviction after release beyond active residential TAU, despite improvement within both arms during residence.

The studies therefore reject both blanket conclusions: neither “family/community treatment lasts” nor “residential treatment washes out” fits all four results.

### What follow-on contact, caregiver training, school coordination, and measurement appear necessary?

The positive studies contain dense infrastructure but do not isolate it.

- MST works across family and community systems, but the 21.9-year source does not identify which original component or later service produced the endpoint.
- MTFC combined trained caregivers, daily data, weekly supervision, family-of-origin therapy, school coordination, on-call support, and professional consultation. The study does not identify a single active ingredient or a standardized post-exit dose.
- FFT included fidelity monitoring, but the active comparator and differing service hours prevent a simple model-versus-no-care interpretation.
- The Swedish module included relapse planning and named prosocial contacts, but it did not deliver a measured continuing-care package and completion was low.

For later research, “transition” must be measured as delivered exposure: named responsible person, start and stop dates, contact frequency, caregiver participation, school or vocational coordination, clinical access, crisis route, fidelity, dropout, and actual uptake. A written plan or anticipated support is not an outcome.

### Which results concern lower-level conduct and which concern serious or repeated violence?

- C-001 concerns serious and violent juvenile offenders with multiple prior felony arrests.
- C-002 primarily concerns disruptive and aggressive child-welfare referrals; very few participants had prior arrests. Its charge outcome mostly tests prevention of onset, not recidivism after serious violence.
- C-003 concerns girls with serious and chronic delinquency and extensive prior court referrals, but its composite combines criminal referrals, confinement, and self-reported delinquency rather than reporting victim-specific violent recurrence.
- C-004 concerns convicted serious violent offenders, but some participants were 18 to 21 and therefore not children.

No result should be moved between these severity and developmental groups without an explicit transfer argument.

### Are victim safety, recurrence, child wellbeing, and family burden measured separately?

Not adequately.

| Source | Recurrence or official conduct | Child or participant wellbeing | Victim safety | Family burden |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| C-001 | felony and misdemeanor records; incarceration | not directly at the 21.9-year endpoint | not measured directly | family-related civil suits are an indirect legal outcome, not burden measurement |
| C-002 | criminal charges; out-of-home placement | not a primary endpoint in this registry paper | not measured | not measured |
| C-003 | criminal referrals, locked-setting days, self-reported delinquency | not a distinct primary endpoint | not measured directly | not measured |
| C-004 | violent and any reconviction; 12-month aggression | limited conduct and aggression measures, not broad wellbeing | not measured directly | not measured |

The shared absence matters. Lower recidivism cannot establish victim recovery, unreported safety, child flourishing, or sustainable family care. Those outcomes require separate instruments, reporters, time windows, and safeguards against one favorable domain canceling a non-waivable harm in another.

## Durable-transition measurement rule

Future evidence should not be called post-treatment or post-exit unless the observation window is anchored to an actual service or placement end. At minimum, record:

1. randomization or comparison condition;
2. baseline conduct and severity without converting diagnosis or identity into danger;
3. actual service start, dose, fidelity, completion, crossover, and dropout;
4. placement or treatment end and release date;
5. every follow-on service, caregiver contact, school or vocational support, and crisis route actually received;
6. conduct-specific recurrence, opportunity and time at risk, and official-record jurisdiction;
7. victim safety and recovery;
8. child wellbeing, autonomy, education, relationships, and material stability;
9. caregiver burden and safety;
10. restriction, confinement, placement disruption, medication, and other treatment costs or harms;
11. missing cases, subgroup estimates, and uncertainty; and
12. outcomes at several fixed intervals after the active support ends.

An in-program pre/post change is not a comparative effect. A treatment plan is not delivered aftercare. Completion is not random. A criminal record is not a complete safety or wellbeing measure.

## Transfer boundary

The four studies support a professional-service design boundary, not a community treatment mandate.

Potentially transferable questions include whether a response has trained caregivers, independent clinical supervision, family and school coordination, conduct-specific records, an active comparator, actual transition support, and separate later outcomes. The evidence does not authorize residents to diagnose, compel therapy, impose custody, run correctional placement, use risk scores for admission or membership, or treat peer disclosure as clinical care.

When a private community helps a family obtain lawful services, its role should remain ordinary and bounded: preserve observable facts, support voluntary access, protect confidentiality, avoid retaliation, maintain school and family contact, and cooperate with independent professionals and statutory authorities. Treatment records and disclosures should remain firewalled from membership, labor, romance, housing, benefits, and discipline decisions.

## Gap consequences without prose change

Unit C strengthens five existing gaps without changing their B, C, or D classifications:

- **G-002 — community as therapist:** durable effects arose, when they arose, inside professional systems with active caregiver, school, clinical, implementation, and record infrastructure; results varied sharply by model and comparator.
- **G-005 — non-waivable rights:** treatment participation or nonresponse does not waive bodily safety, family contact, schooling, independent care, privacy, reply, or appeal.
- **G-006 — outcome dashboard:** treatment window, release date, continuing services, completion, crossover, official jurisdiction, recurrence, victim safety, wellbeing, and family burden must be separate fields.
- **G-009 — collective childrearing:** family-style care is not self-validating; the positive foster-care trial used state certification, training, daily monitoring, weekly supervision, family therapy, school coordination, and external records.
- **G-018 — limits of screening:** response, completion, conviction, disruptive behavior, and treatment-group averages do not validate an admission, personality, or dangerousness filter.
