## A001 — Do not call a prevented outcome a false alarm
**Status:** TAIL-PROVISIONAL + PRACTICAL  
**Creative Tail:** C017

If a community forecasts a serious failure and that forecast triggers a successful rescue, the post-rescue outcome is not a clean label for whether the forecast was accurate.

Example: a community forecasts high insolvency risk **without new federation aid**; the warning triggers emergency liquidity; insolvency is prevented. Recording `predicted failure; failure did not occur` would punish successful prevention and can chill early warning.

Operational rule:

1. state the intervention regime assumed by the forecast;
2. timestamp/freeze the forecast and evidence before the aid decision;
3. evaluate forecast quality separately from intervention efficacy;
4. distinguish `prevented event`, `poor forecast`, `intervention probably irrelevant`, and `counterfactual unresolved` rather than binary right/wrong scoring;
5. evaluate calibration across repeated probabilistic forecasts where feasible.

Do not turn counterfactual estimates into a moral score. Need, responsibility, preventability, rights, and forecast accuracy are separate questions.

## A002 — Record rescue/subsidy exposure when comparing community robustness
**Status:** PRACTICAL / KNOWN + RESEARCH-CONTROL

A community that repeatedly survives because a federation supplies liquidity, staff, debt relief, mediation, land, food, or other rescue can look intrinsically robust if support exposure is omitted.

When comparing survival or reproduction, distinguish:

- unaided operation;
- ordinary standing federation services;
- exceptional rescue;
- recurring subsidy;
- one-time restructuring.

This does not make supported survival illegitimate. It prevents the support system from being mistaken for a property of the local design alone.

## A003 — Forecast risk under an explicit intervention regime
**Status:** PRACTICAL / KNOWN

`80% risk` is incomplete. Record whether it means risk:

- without additional federation help;
- under current support;
- under a named rescue package;
- of crossing a defined trigger.

This makes later learning possible and reduces narrative rewriting after the outcome is known.

## A004 — Use self-disclosure protection carefully to preserve early warning
**Status:** PRACTICAL / KNOWN

Where communities are punished merely for honestly disclosing a control failure, they have incentives to hide the problem until it is harder to fix.

Consider bounded procedural/penalty protection for prompt self-reporting where appropriate, while preserving restitution, affected-person rights, evidence, and accountability for intentional harm. Self-disclosure must not buy immunity.

## A005 — Size mutual-aid reserves for correlated shocks
**Status:** PRACTICAL / KNOWN

Do not size a federation rescue/solidarity fund as if community claims were independent. Drought, recession, epidemic, legal attack, insurance withdrawal, supplier failure, transport disruption, or platform loss can hit many communities simultaneously.

Use plausible common-shock/stress scenarios as well as average historical claim rates.

## A006 — Avoid first-come depletion of a scarce rescue pool
**Status:** PRACTICAL / KNOWN

When several communities may need a fixed fund simultaneously, first-come allocation can reward speed, administrative sophistication, or strategic early declaration rather than need or expected benefit.

Predefine triage/allocation rules and preserve emergency exceptions rather than discovering the rule after the fund is nearly empty.

## A007 — Stress-test rights/remedies under correlated invocation
**Status:** PRACTICAL / KNOWN  
**Related Creative Tail:** C015; Batch 24

A right can be procedurally live for one claimant while becoming practically dead when many people need it at once.

Examples:

- one independent appeal panel receives complaints from several communities after a movement-wide scandal;
- emergency exit housing is adequate for one household but not ten;
- one records officer becomes the bottleneck during a mass separation;
- one federation mediator/advocate cannot handle simultaneous local breakdowns.

Extend rights-liveness review beyond `is there a path?` to `does that path remain usable under a plausible surge?`

Stress:

- reviewer/adjudicator capacity;
- response-time bounds;
- emergency housing and liquidity;
- advocacy/translation capacity;
- record access and preservation;
- alternate/overflow jurisdiction;
- common-shock case volume.

If a route saturates, define overflow, escalation or temporary additional capacity before crisis.

## A008 — A federation bypass can itself be a single point of failure
**Status:** PRACTICAL / KNOWN

Do not count five local appeal routes as five independent protections if all five terminate at the same understaffed, conflicted, or technologically dependent federation body.

Map both logical independence and shared capacity/control dependencies.

## A009 — Map federation shared-service blast radius
**Status:** PRACTICAL / KNOWN

Federated efficiency can quietly create common-mode dependence through one:

- accountant or treasury system;
- identity/authentication service;
- cloud/document platform;
- insurer;
- landholding vehicle;
- transport/logistics hub;
- records archive;
- mediator/ombuds team;
- legal/medical specialist;
- communications system.

Ask what one failure or capture event would disable across otherwise separate communities. Build alternative routes where the failure would threaten necessities, rights, or movement continuity.

## A010 — Record and review near misses
**Status:** PRACTICAL / KNOWN

Do not learn only from realized disasters. Preserve events where a serious failure was narrowly avoided because someone noticed, improvised, refused, escalated, or an external condition happened to break the chain.

A useful near-miss record separates:

- initiating condition;
- failure path that almost completed;
- intervention/barrier that stopped it;
- whether that barrier was designed or accidental;
- whether the same barrier would work next time.

Near misses are signals, not proof that disaster was inevitable.

## A011 — Correct for administrative/legibility bias in rescue allocation
**Status:** PRACTICAL / KNOWN

The community most able to document its distress, complete forms, quantify losses, and communicate with federation staff may not be the one with the greatest need.

When allocating scarce support, examine whether application/measurement requirements systematically favor high-administration communities. Provide bounded assistance for evidence formation where needed without abandoning verification.

## A012 — Cohort admission has applicant-waiting costs
**Status:** PRACTICAL / KNOWN  
**Related Creative Tail:** C009

Cohort-composition admission is useful only where candidate market thickness makes batching realistic. If applicants will not wait, delaying decisions can cause matching-market unraveling: good candidates accept other opportunities before the cohort can be assembled.

For founding/expansion waves, compare the expected composition benefit against:

- vacancy cost;
- applicant waiting cost;
- probability of applicant loss;
- synchronization feasibility;
- cost of maintaining reversible/provisional offers.

Do not batch merely because cohort optimization sounds mathematically superior.

## A013 — Shared experts can transmit failure as well as competence
**Status:** PRACTICAL / KNOWN

Roving founders, mediators, therapists, trainers, accountants, lawyers, administrators, or technical experts can spread best practices across a federation—but can also propagate one bad assumption, conflict, capture relationship, or procedural error widely.

For high-leverage shared experts:

- preserve independent review;
- avoid one-person monopoly;
- record conflicts and role dependencies;
- compare outcomes across communities;
- keep local capacity from atrophying entirely;
- have a replacement route if the shared expert becomes unavailable or implicated.
