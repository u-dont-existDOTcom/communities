# Main lesson — Rotate authority without repeatedly resetting competence to zero

**Status:** PRACTICAL / KNOWN + GOVERNANCE DESIGN

Role rotation can reduce entrenchment, but if every formal officeholder is repeatedly replaced by a novice while the same trainer, records custodian, technical expert or adviser persists, formal rotation can shift power into an informal shadow rather than distribute it.

Twin Oaks already uses rotating Planners alongside longer-lived managers and recommends training backups for management; cooperative research on the Brazilian Landless Workers Movement documents wider job rotation as a response to function-based informal hierarchy. Political term-limit research shows the related effect of rapid formal turnover increasing reliance on durable staff/lobbyists.

---

## B50-L01 — Separate authority from expertise

For every critical role identify separately:
- final decision authority;
- technical skill;
- execution;
- records/custody;
- training/mentoring.

These do not need identical turnover schedules.

## B50-L02 — Require overlap and demonstrated handoff

Before full transfer:
- incoming person performs core tasks;
- outgoing person observes/corrects;
- access/records are transferred;
- emergency backup is identified;
- unresolved decisions are documented.

## B50-L03 — Audit durable shadow actors

Map who repeatedly:
- trains successors;
- explains policy history;
- controls records/passwords;
- prepares data/agendas;
- advises rotating leaders.

Such continuity can be valuable, but its influence must be visible and bounded.

## B50-L04 — Use multi-source institutional memory

Avoid one permanent expert becoming the only explanation of how a system works.

Use:
- readable procedures;
- accessible decision histories;
- C018 governance tombstones;
- multiple trained backups;
- cross-training;
- shared records.

## B50-L05 — Match rotation interval to learning curve and capture risk

Shorter tenure can make sense where:
- authority is coercive/high-capture;
- role is quickly learned.

Longer or overlapped tenure can make sense where:
- competence takes time;
- error costs are high;
- institutional memory matters.

## B50-L06 — Keep stable technical experts non-sovereign where possible

Stable accountants, clinicians, engineers, IT/records specialists or other experts can preserve competence without receiving final policy/governance authority outside their technical remit.

## B50-L07 — Do not let trainers monopolize successor selection

If the same expert trains every successor and decides who qualifies, the training role can become an appointment gate.

Separate training, evaluation and appointment where practical.

## B50-L08 — Test backups

A backup named in a document is not a functioning backup.

Periodically demonstrate under bounded conditions that another person can perform essential tasks.

## B50-L09 — Preserve accountability across rotation

For decisions whose consequences arrive after the officeholder leaves, preserve:
- initiating decision;
- rationale/evidence;
- predicted outcomes;
- later outcome review.

Rotation should not erase responsibility/learning.

## B50-L10 — Audit access equity to rotating roles

Training/time burdens can systematically exclude:
- caregivers;
- disabled members;
- people with less schedule flexibility;
- newer but otherwise qualified members.

Formal openness is not enough if role preparation is inaccessible.

## B50-L11 — Cross-functional rotation can reduce opaque departmental hierarchy

Short placements/learning exchanges across functions can improve mutual understanding without forcing permanent reassignment or compulsory amateurism.

## B50-L12 — Do not count turnover itself as egalitarian success

Measure actual:
- influence distribution;
- dependence on durable advisers;
- knowledge/record access;
- succession competence;
- control of credentials and information.

A community can rotate titles while leaving informal hierarchy unchanged.
